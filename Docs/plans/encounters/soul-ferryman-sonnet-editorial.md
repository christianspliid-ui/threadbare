# Encounter Pipeline: Soul Ferryman
> Scale: short | Slug: soul-ferryman-sonnet | Pass: editorial
> Date: 2026-04-05 | Pipeline version: 1.0

---

## 1. Prose Quality

The opening paragraph is excellent. The fog that does not move, the detail of "cloth dragged through slow water," the bureaucrat pretending he is not waiting -- this is scene-first writing with real cadence. The second paragraph is nearly as strong: "a woman, or what had been a woman, still wearing the specific posture of someone who died moving, who died *toward* something." That italicized *toward* earns its emphasis. The closing line -- "You're early," he said, which was a strange thing to say to a god" -- lands with exactly the right weight.

**Branch A (Negotiate the Crossing)** is the weaker of the two branch paragraphs. The opening sentence -- "You spoke to him in the language of exchange, which is the language he has always used" -- is good but abstract. The next sentence is the problem:

> "You offered something -- not coin, not prayer, something with actual weight in it"

This is a placeholder wearing scene-prose clothing. What did the god offer? The vagueness is deliberate (the system will fill it), but the prose does not earn the elision. It reads as if the author did not know what to put there and dressed the gap in dashes. The tradesman simile ("the way a tradesman looks at a fair price") is competent but not vivid enough to carry the paragraph past the vague offering.

Cassia's backward look in Branch A is strong -- "Her expression was not angry. It was something quieter and less resolvable than anger" -- that is good writing. But the paragraph arrives at that moment too quickly because the middle is thin.

**Branch B (Dissolve the Contract)** is noticeably stronger. "The extraction reversed like thread pulled from a needle" is a clean image. Morden's response -- "That was mine. You understand I have to write that down" -- is the best character moment in the entire encounter. The ledger detail ("three strokes of his pen") is specific and eerie. The closing image of Vorlen turning up his collar and walking away empty-handed is excellent atmospheric prose.

**Aftermath paragraph** is solid but sags slightly in the middle. This sentence is overwritten:

> "The dead move through economies you can sense but not audit, and what the living spent tonight on her behalf -- the arrangement with the man in the coat, the quiet price of keeping powerful things unremembered -- simply didn't work out."

The parenthetical interruption ("the arrangement with the man in the coat, the quiet price of keeping powerful things unremembered") is doing summary work that the scene already did. The player watched Vorlen. They do not need a recap. The sentence would be stronger cut to: "The dead move through economies you can sense but not audit, and what the living spent tonight on her behalf simply didn't work out."

The closing line -- "You know the ferryman's name now, which is not nothing" -- is a perfect short-scale landing.

**[EDITORIAL REWRITE] -- Branch A, middle passage:**

Original:
> You spoke to him in the language of exchange, which is the language he has always used. You offered something -- not coin, not prayer, something with actual weight in it -- and he looked at you the way a tradesman looks at a fair price: without pleasure, without complaint, with the particular respect that attends a transaction conducted honestly.

Rewrite:
> You spoke to him in the language of exchange, which is the language he has always used. You set something on the rail of his boat -- not coin, not prayer, but a sliver of attention, a fragment of the weight a god carries without noticing until they put it down. He looked at it, then at you, with the particular respect that attends a transaction conducted honestly. No pleasure. No complaint. The ferryman is not a creature who haggles.

## 2. Branch Seduction Audit

### Branch A: Negotiate the Crossing (Bargain)

- **Fantasy of interference:** Being a god who speaks the old language. Treating the ferryman as a legitimate party rather than an obstacle. The fantasy is diplomatic mastery across a cosmic power gap -- not condescension, but recognition.
- **Why would a god choose this?** Because it establishes a relationship. Because a god who bargains with Morden gains standing in the river's economy. Because the player who chooses this is saying: *the world has its structures and I will work within them when they are honest*.
- **Value it protects:** Legitimacy. Order. The coherence of the world's transactional systems.
- **Moral asymmetry:** The player pays a personal cost but avoids the River Debt mark. This is the "cleaner" path -- but only because it concedes Morden's authority, which is its own kind of cost that the prose does not surface strongly enough.
- **Dramatic asymmetry:** Lower voltage than Branch B. The scene resolves quietly. The boat moves. This is a feature, not a bug -- but the prose needs to work harder to make quietness feel like payoff rather than anticlimax.
- **Information asymmetry:** Identical to Branch B at the success level. On critical_success, the intelligence fragment (another soul in transit) is available on both branches, so no information asymmetry exists between branches. This is fine for short scale.
- **Prose asymmetry:** Branch A is currently underauthored relative to Branch B. See Section 1. Branch B has Morden's ledger moment; Branch A has no equivalent character beat for Morden. The tradesman simile is not enough.
- **Aftermath asymmetry:** Branch A avoids River Debt; Branch B acquires it. This is real and meaningful. But the aftermath paragraph is identical for both branches -- it does not differentiate. The aftermath prose should acknowledge which path was taken. Currently it reads as a universal closing that floats above the branch choice. This is a problem.

### Branch B: Dissolve the Contract (Break)

- **Fantasy of interference:** Being a god who draws a line. The fantasy is principled force -- not rage, not destruction, but the unilateral statement that some transactions should not exist.
- **Why would a god choose this?** Because the god believes the dead have sovereignty over their own memories. Because Morden's "right" to extract payment is itself the injustice. Because sometimes a god acts not from negotiation but from conviction.
- **Value it protects:** Sovereignty. The integrity of the dead. The principle that death-in-service should not be followed by posthumous exploitation.
- **Moral asymmetry:** The player acts unilaterally and acquires River Debt. This is the "louder" path. Morden does not fight back -- he notes. The cost is future-facing and atmospheric rather than immediate.
- **Dramatic asymmetry:** Higher voltage. Morden's silence, then his ledger notation, is the encounter's strongest scene beat.
- **Prose asymmetry:** Stronger prose. See Section 1.

**Branch seduction verdict:** Both branches are genuinely tempting for different reasons. Neither is obviously correct. The Bargain/Break template is working as intended. However, the prose quality gap between Branch A and Branch B undermines the balance -- a player reading both will feel Branch B is "more authored," which biases the choice. Branch A needs the rewrite suggested above.

## 3. Branch Count Assessment

Two branches for a short-scale encounter is correct. The draft explicitly considered and rejected a third branch (Bind -- containment), arguing it does not earn its place at short scale. This is the right call. A Bind branch would require a second beat to develop the containment arc, which would violate scale discipline.

The two branches are structurally distinct (exchange vs. unilateral act), tonally distinct (quiet resolution vs. charged silence), and consequentially distinct (no River Debt vs. River Debt). Each earns its place.

**Assessment: PASS.**

## 4. Scale Discipline Check

Short scale declares: one meaningful beat + aftermath. The draft delivers exactly this. Beat 1 contains the approach, recognition, and choice. The aftermath is compact. No second beat is attempted.

The pressure knot is appropriately scoped -- one soul, one ferryman, one faction operative, one contested memory. The stakes are local and atmospheric, not world-shaping. The justification in Section 2 is honest: inflating this to medium would claim the ferryman is more cosmically significant than the Threadbare aesthetic warrants.

The support bundle is slightly ambitious for short scale -- Morden as a `must-persist` NPC with future encounter hooks, follow-on encounter seeds on failure outcomes, intelligence fragments on critical_success. This is at the upper edge of what short scale should carry. It does not violate scale discipline because the complexity is in the *consequence* layer, not the *scene* layer -- the encounter itself is compact; the persistence contracts are appropriately deferred.

**Assessment: PASS with note.** The persistence layer is dense for short scale. This is acceptable only because the scene layer is genuinely compact. If future short encounters trend toward this density of follow-on hooks, that is scope creep disguised as aftermath design.

## 5. Inspiration Anchor Honesty

The anchors are not decorative. They materially shaped the encounter:

- **The Impossible Bargain** is structural -- the encounter's entire dilemma grammar ("every crossing costs; refusing to pay costs differently") comes directly from this archetype. Without it, this would be a rescue encounter. With it, rescue is not on the table -- only the question of who pays.
- **The Strategic Immortal** changed the ferryman from a monster into a function. This is the encounter's most important architectural decision. A monstrous ferryman produces a combat-or-diplomacy binary. A functional ferryman produces a legitimacy-or-sovereignty dilemma. The archetype earned its citation.
- **Anti-pattern avoidance (Dark Lord, Clean Moral Binaries, Player as Savior)** is genuine and visible in the prose. Morden is not evil. Cassia is not innocent. The player does not save her -- she crosses regardless.
- **Descent Into Darkness** is the weakest anchor. The fog imagery is atmospheric but "descent" is a stretch for an encounter that takes place entirely on a riverbank. The fog narrows visibility but does not produce the psychological compression that a true descent archetype implies. This anchor is present in flavor but not in structure.

**Assessment: PASS.** Four of five anchors are load-bearing. The Descent anchor is decorative but not dishonest -- it describes atmosphere, not structure, and the draft does not claim otherwise.

## 6. Aftermath Payoff

The aftermath paragraph lands. "Cassia Felt is gone. She crossed with something intact" is actor-centered. The closing line about knowing the ferryman's name is the right note for short scale -- modest, specific, forward-facing.

**Problem:** The aftermath is not branch-sensitive. Both branches produce the same aftermath paragraph. The player who bargained and the player who broke the contract read the same closing text. This is a significant gap. The aftermath should acknowledge:
- For Bargain: the quiet sense of a transaction completed, the absence of River Debt, the feeling of having engaged Morden on his terms.
- For Break: the weight of the ledger notation, the presence of River Debt, the feeling of having drawn a line that Morden will remember.

The aftermath kit summary (Section 14) differentiates correctly at the system level (River Debt applied or not), but the *prose* aftermath does not. For an encounter whose entire thesis is that the two branches produce different kinds of cost, this is a meaningful omission.

The lack of aftermath reaction choices is correctly justified for short scale. The Section 13 explanation is honest.

**Assessment: REVISE.** The aftermath paragraph must be branched or at minimum include a branch-conditional sentence. A shared aftermath that ignores the branch choice undermines the encounter's central promise.

## 7. Dilemma Energy

The dilemma is real. The encounter avoids the most common failure mode of Bargain/Bind/Break encounters (identified in the branching templates document): "break becomes the obviously practical path and bargain becomes cosmetic weirdness."

Both paths preserve Cassia's memory. The fork is about cost and relationship, not outcome. This is a genuine dilemma because:
- Bargain is not weaker -- it avoids River Debt and establishes a transactional relationship with an ancient force.
- Break is not stronger -- it acquires River Debt and establishes a confrontational relationship.
- Neither path is obviously "right" for a god. A god of Memory might bargain (speaking Memory's own language) or break (asserting that memory belongs to the dead, not to ferrymen). A god of Power might bargain (demonstrating mastery of the exchange) or break (demonstrating sovereignty over lesser forces).

The dilemma energy is good. The one weakness is that both branches produce the same outcome for Cassia (she crosses intact), which means the player's choice is entirely about self-expression and future consequence, not about immediate stakes. This is appropriate for short scale but would be insufficient for medium or long.

**Assessment: PASS.**

## 9. Verdict

**REVISE BEFORE CONTINUING.**

The encounter is strong. The prose is frequently excellent. The dilemma is genuine. The branching template is well-chosen and well-applied. Morden is a memorable character. The scale discipline is good.

The encounter fails on one critical point: the aftermath prose does not differentiate between branches. This is not a minor polish issue -- it is the encounter's final moment, the last thing the player reads, and it currently ignores the most important thing the player did. For an encounter whose entire thesis is that two different kinds of cost produce two different kinds of relationship with an ancient force, a shared aftermath that mentions neither cost nor relationship is a structural gap.

Secondary issues: Branch A prose is underauthored relative to Branch B, and the aftermath paragraph has a recap sentence that should be cut.

None of these require structural redesign. The encounter's architecture is sound. The revision is prose-level work.

---

## 10. Revision Summary

### Must Fix

1. **Branch the aftermath prose.** Write two aftermath variants (or a shared paragraph with branch-conditional sentences) that acknowledge whether the player bargained or broke the contract. The River Debt mark and the transactional relationship are the encounter's two distinct endings -- the prose must reflect them.

2. **Strengthen Branch A prose.** The "something with actual weight in it" elision needs specificity. Use the editorial rewrite provided in Section 1 or write an equivalent that gives the offered payment a concrete image. Branch A also needs a Morden character beat comparable to Branch B's ledger moment -- Morden is the encounter's strongest character, and he nearly disappears in Branch A.

### Should Fix

3. **Cut the recap sentence in the aftermath.** Remove "the arrangement with the man in the coat, the quiet price of keeping powerful things unremembered" from the aftermath paragraph. The player watched Vorlen. They do not need summary.

4. **Add a concept art recommendation.** The draft contains no concept art suggestion. The fog-shrouded river, the boat, and the ferryman with his ledger are strongly visual. A concept art recommendation should be present for the art direction pass.

### Consider

5. **Branch A -- Morden's farewell.** Consider giving Morden a single spoken line in Branch A that mirrors the ledger moment in Branch B. Something transactional and slightly eerie -- not warm, not hostile, just the ferryman acknowledging a completed exchange. This would balance the character presence across branches.

6. **Aftermath -- Vorlen's departure.** The aftermath mentions Vorlen obliquely ("Someone on a high floor of somewhere"). Consider a single direct image of Vorlen leaving -- Branch B has this ("the man in the official's coat checked the sky and turned up his collar"), but the aftermath does not. A brief callback would ground the closing.

# Encounter Pipeline: Soul Ferryman

> Scale: short | Slug: soul-ferryman-opus | Pass: revised
> Revisions applied: REV-01 (community stakes), REV-02 (felt absence), REV-03 (simile variety)
> Date: 2026-04-05 | Pipeline version: 1.0

---

## 1. Inspiration Anchors

**Foundation references:**

- **Thematic Pillars** -- "Compassion vs. Power" is the primary axis. The ferryman's toll is not violence; it is a transaction, and the player must decide whether to intervene in an economy that preys on desperation. "Ascension vs. Humanity / Sovereignty vs. Consumption" is the secondary axis: the ferryman is a mortal who has become an instrument of something older than himself, and his bargain with the river is a small-scale mirror of the parasitic logic that ascendancy risks. His passengers lose something of themselves to cross; the question is whether that loss is exploitation or fair exchange in a world where nothing moves for free.
- **Anti-Patterns** -- Actively avoiding #1 (Dark Lord Problem): the ferryman is not evil. He is a man bound to a contract he made when he was younger and more desperate, and the river holds him to it. Avoiding #3 (Consequence-Free Magic): the soul-toll is real and textured -- passengers do not lose a game resource called "soul points," they lose a quality of selfhood that changes how they walk through the world afterward. Avoiding #9 (Clean Moral Binaries): stopping the ferryman means stranding everyone who needs to cross. The river has no bridge. The next ford is four days downstream. Avoiding #10 (Player as Savior): the ferryman has been operating for decades. Travelers have been paying his toll and surviving, diminished but alive. This is not a crisis the player rescues anyone from -- it is a condition the player decides whether to disturb.
- **Ordeal Archetypes -- "The Impossible Bargain"** -- The encounter draws from the Impossible Bargain archetype: every option costs something irreplaceable, the terms are honest, and the tension is cold clarity rather than dramatic confrontation. The ferryman's toll is not hidden or deceptive. He states it plainly. The travelers know what they are paying. The question is whether the player-god considers this toll acceptable in a world full of worse ones.
- **Character Archetypes -- "The Wandering Scholar-Monster"** -- The ferryman draws from this archetype's core tension: ancient knowledge and isolation versus hunger for connection. He is not ancient, but he has been alone on the river for thirty years, and the fog has made him strange. He knows the names of the drowned. He speaks to the current. He is monstrous in the way that long solitude in a liminal place makes anyone monstrous -- not through cruelty, but through the erosion of ordinary human reference points.

**Dilemma Library:** Not formally consulted, but the encounter's two-branch structure naturally produces dilemma energy: intervening to break the bargain protects future travelers but strands current ones and destroys a man who is already half-consumed by what he serves. Letting it stand means accepting a small predation as the price of passage in a world where rivers do not care about fairness.

**Anti-patterns actively avoided:**
- Dark Lord Problem (the ferryman is pitiable, not villainous)
- Consequence-Free Magic (the soul-toll produces real, lasting change in those who pay it)
- Clean Moral Binaries (stopping the ferryman creates a different kind of harm)
- Player as Savior (the situation has persisted for decades; travelers have coped)
- Generic Fantasy Aesthetics (the fog, the river, the ferryman's pole-worn hands are specific and sensory)

## 2. Scale Justification

This is a **short** encounter because it is a liminal moment, not a crisis. A god-being notices a transaction at a fog-shrouded river crossing and decides whether to intervene. There is no siege, no faction war, no cascading political consequence. The encounter's weight is atmospheric and moral: it asks what kind of god the player is when confronted with a small, honest predation that has been running quietly for years. One intervention beat with two branches captures this cleanly. Inflating it to medium would require inventing stakeholders and faction pressure that would dilute the encounter's real strength, which is its intimacy -- one ferryman, one passenger, one river, one god watching from the fog.

## 3. Pressure Knot

The river is called the Silt, or sometimes just the Grey, and nobody alive remembers which name came first. It runs through a notch between two limestone ridges where the fog collects every morning and does not burn off until midday, and some days it does not burn off at all. The only crossing for twenty miles is a flat-bottomed punt operated by a man named Vesik, who has poled travelers across this stretch of water for thirty-one years.

Vesik does not charge coin. He charges something else. When a traveler steps onto his punt, he looks at them with fog-pale eyes and names a quality -- courage, tenderness, the memory of a particular summer, the capacity to trust strangers -- and if they agree to pay it, he poles them across. When they step off on the far bank, the quality is gone. Not suppressed. Gone. They remember having it the way one remembers a room in a house that has burned down. The shape is there. The warmth is not.

He is not a sorcerer. He made a bargain with the river when he was twenty-three and drowning, and the river gave him back his life and the punt and the fog, and in return he collects. He does not keep what he takes. It goes into the current. He is a mechanism, not a thief. He knows this. It does not comfort him.

A hamlet called Siltside depends on the crossing -- a dozen families, a bonesetter who serves both banks, a twice-monthly grain barter that keeps the downstream farms fed through wet season. They pay the toll. They have always paid the toll. The bonesetter lost her sense of direction years ago and navigates by memory alone; the grain-trader cannot recall his wife's laugh but remembers the weight of every sack he has carried. The crossing is a wound the community has learned to live around.

Right now, a young courier named Diehl stands at the near bank with a satchel of sealed letters for a garrison commander on the far side. He has been standing there for an hour, listening to Vesik explain the toll. Diehl's horse will not board the punt; it smells the fog and refuses. The next ford is four days downstream, and the letters are expected tomorrow. Diehl is afraid, and he has not yet decided.

The player's agent is nearby -- arriving at the crossing, or watching from the ridge above.

## 4. Intervention Fantasy

The player is a god looking down through fog at a transaction that is not quite wrong and not quite right. A man who owes his life to a river is collecting a toll that diminishes everyone who pays it, and a young courier is about to lose something he does not fully understand in order to deliver letters he has not read. The fantasy is not rescue. It is judgment -- the quiet, enormous kind that gods exercise when they decide whether a thing in their world should continue. The player can reach into this moment and break the bargain, freeing Vesik and closing the crossing, or they can steady the courier and let the transaction complete, accepting that the world contains ferrymen like this and rivers like this and tolls like this. Either way, the god is making a statement about what they will permit in the territory of their influence. The compelling part is that neither option is clean. Breaking the bargain is an act of mercy toward future travelers and an act of destruction toward Vesik, who will lose his purpose and possibly his life. Steadying the courier is an act of pragmatism that ratifies a small predation. The god is not choosing between good and evil. The god is choosing what kind of world they are building.

## 5. Cast and World Objects

**Primary NPCs:**
- **Vesik** -- ferryman, early fifties, gaunt, fog-bleached. Hands calloused from thirty-one years on the punt pole. Speaks in a flat, careful voice. Not hostile. Not warm. Bound to the river by a drowning-bargain he made at twenty-three. Collects soul-qualities as toll; does not keep them.
- **Diehl** -- courier, early twenties. Carries sealed military correspondence. Reliable, slightly anxious. Has never crossed the Silt before. Standing at the near bank deciding whether to pay.

**Supporting NPCs:**
- **The river itself** -- not an NPC in a mechanical sense, but the fog-shrouded Silt is the real power in this encounter. It holds Vesik's contract. It receives what he collects. It does not negotiate.

**Involved factions/communities:**
- **The garrison on the far bank** -- the destination for Diehl's letters. Not present, but their expectations create the courier's time pressure.
- **Local travelers** -- the crossing's regular users. They know the toll. Most pay it. Some go four days downstream to the ford instead. A small, worn economy of diminishment.

**Required places:**
- **The Silt crossing** -- a fog-shrouded river notch between limestone ridges. A wooden dock on each bank, weathered grey. The punt is flat-bottomed, large enough for three people and no horses.
- **The near bank** -- where Diehl and his horse wait. A muddy track, a hitching post, a cairn of river stones that travelers have stacked over the years.

**Required reward objects / burdens / attachments:**
- On "Break the Bargain" path: Vesik is freed from the river-contract but loses his livelihood and his connection to the crossing. The crossing becomes impassable until a bridge or new ferry is established. Mark on Vesik: "Unbound" -- a condition of disoriented freedom.
- On "Steady the Courier" path: Diehl crosses and loses a soul-quality (named at resolution time by the ferryman). Mark on Diehl: "Diminished" -- a permanent, subtle reduction. He delivers the letters. The crossing persists.
- On either path: the player's agent gains or loses minor reputation with local travelers depending on outcome tier.

**Reputation / social-state channels:**
- Local traveler community disposition (regional reputation for the crossing area)
- Vesik's personal disposition toward the player's agent (if he survives the encounter)
- Diehl's personal disposition (gratitude, resentment, or numbness depending on path and outcome)

## 6. Beat Structure

**Beat 1 -- The Crossing (single beat, short encounter)**

The player's agent encounters the standoff at the river crossing. Vesik waits on his punt. Diehl hesitates on the bank. The fog is thick enough to muffle sound. The encounter presents two intervention paths:

- **Break the Bargain** -- the player reaches into the fog and severs the contract between Vesik and the river. The toll ends. The crossing closes. Vesik is unbound.
- **Steady the Courier** -- the player lends Diehl the resolve to step onto the punt and pay the toll. The crossing continues. Diehl loses a quality of himself. The letters are delivered.

Resolution is determined by the outcome ladder. There is no second beat -- the aftermath handles consequences.

## 7. Branching Profile

- **Branch depth:** `light`
- **Branch count:** `2`
- **Where branching lives:** `choice set`, `outcome ladder`, `aftermath`
- **Convergence policy:** `stays divergent through aftermath` -- the two paths produce fundamentally different world states (crossing open vs. crossing closed; Vesik bound vs. Vesik free; Diehl whole vs. Diehl diminished).
- **Primary branching template:** `Bargain / Bind / Break` (template #3) -- the player can negotiate with the force (steady the courier, accept the bargain's terms), or break it (sever the contract). The "bind" axis is implicit: the river's contract is already a binding, and the player decides whether to sustain or destroy it.

**Why this template:** The encounter is fundamentally about a mystical bargain. The Bargain / Bind / Break grammar naturally fits: the player either accepts the bargain's logic (steady the courier) or breaks it (sever the contract). The template's usual failure mode -- "break becomes the obviously practical path" -- is avoided because breaking the bargain destroys the crossing and harms Vesik.

## 8. Branching Map

**Branch A: Break the Bargain**
- Scene prose: the god reaches into the fog and the river-contract snaps. Vesik staggers. The punt drifts. The fog begins to thin.
- Outcome ladder emphasis: Vesik's fate, the crossing's closure, regional consequence.
- Aftermath: the crossing is gone. Travelers must use the downstream ford (four days). Vesik is alive but purposeless. Diehl is whole but stranded.

**Branch B: Steady the Courier**
- Scene prose: the god leans into Diehl's fear and steadies it. Diehl steps onto the punt. Vesik names the toll.
- Outcome ladder emphasis: what Diehl loses, how cleanly the transaction completes, whether the god's touch is noticed.
- Aftermath: the crossing persists. Diehl delivers his letters, diminished. Vesik continues. The world contains one more small predation.

## 9. Outcome Ladder

### Branch A: Break the Bargain

**Critical Success:**
The contract dissolves cleanly. Vesik collapses on the punt as the binding lifts, but he survives. The fog thins for the first time in thirty years; sunlight touches the river surface and the limestone ridges. The punt drifts to the near bank and lodges in the mud. Vesik sits up, blinking, and begins to weep -- not from grief but from the sudden, overwhelming sensation of being an ordinary man again. Diehl helps him to shore. The crossing is closed, but word reaches the garrison and the nearest settlement within days, and plans for a proper bridge begin. The player's agent gains positive reputation with local travelers ("the god who broke the ferryman's curse") and with Vesik personally. Diehl is whole and delivers his letters via the downstream ford, late but intact.

**Progress:** River-contract severed. Vesik freed. Crossing closed but bridge-planning seeded.
**Spent:** Moderate divine essence.
**New opening:** Bridge-building becomes a future encounter hook. Vesik may reappear as a changed man seeking purpose. The river is no longer predatory at this crossing.

**Success:**
The contract breaks, but roughly. Vesik is thrown from the punt into the shallows. He survives but is disoriented -- the binding was load-bearing for his sense of self, and without it he is a man who has spent thirty-one years as a mechanism suddenly returned to ordinary consciousness. The fog persists for several days before gradually thinning. The crossing is closed. Diehl is whole but must take the four-day detour. The garrison receives its letters late. Local travelers are relieved that the toll is gone but anxious about the lost crossing.

**Progress:** Contract severed. Vesik freed but shaken.
**Spent:** Moderate divine essence.
**New opening:** Vesik needs help reintegrating. The crossing gap creates logistics pressure on the region.

**Success at Cost:**
The contract breaks, but the river resists. The fog surges thick and cold, and something in the current pulls at Vesik as if the river wants to reclaim what it gave him thirty-one years ago. Vesik survives, but barely -- he is pulled from the water by Diehl, coughing silt, and he does not speak for three days afterward. The punt is dragged downstream and lost. The crossing is violently closed. Local travelers are frightened -- the god who broke the ferryman's curse also broke something in the river, and the Silt runs colder and stranger for a season. Diehl is whole but shaken.

**Progress:** Contract severed at visible cost. Vesik alive but damaged. Crossing destroyed, not merely closed.
**Spent:** Moderate divine essence. Regional unease. Vesik's health.
**New burden:** The river is disturbed. The region remembers that divine intervention was violent, not gentle. Vesik may not recover fully.

**Failure:**
The god reaches for the contract and cannot find it. The river's bargain is older and deeper than expected, or the god's influence is insufficient to sever it cleanly. The fog swallows the intervention without effect. Vesik looks up, briefly, as if he felt a wind that was not wind, and then returns to waiting. Diehl is still standing on the bank. Nothing has changed. The player's agent loses minor essence with nothing to show for it.

**Progress:** None. Status quo preserved.
**Spent:** Minor divine essence.
**New burden:** The god has tested the river and found it resistant. Future attempts to break the contract may require more power or a different approach.

**Critical Failure:**
The intervention alerts the river. The fog thickens to opacity. The contract tightens around Vesik -- he gasps, grabs the punt pole, and his eyes go flat and strange. For the first time in thirty-one years, Vesik stops being a reluctant tollkeeper and becomes an active one. He calls out to Diehl in a voice that is not quite his own, offering passage for free -- just this once, just step aboard. The river is hungry and the god's meddling has woken its appetite. If Diehl boards, he loses more than a quality; he loses time, memory, orientation. The crossing becomes more dangerous than before. Vesik is deeper in the river's grip. The player has made everything worse.

**Progress:** Negative. The predation has intensified.
**Spent:** Minor divine essence. Vesik's remaining autonomy. Diehl's safety.
**New burden:** The Silt crossing is now actively dangerous rather than merely costly. Vesik is less human than before. The river remembers being tested.

---

### Branch B: Steady the Courier

**Critical Success:**
The god's touch is precise and warm. Diehl feels his fear settle into something manageable -- not courage exactly, but clarity. He steps onto the punt. Vesik looks at him and names the toll: "Your certainty that you are doing the right thing." Diehl pays it. He crosses. When he steps off on the far bank, he is changed -- he will question his orders more often, hesitate where he once moved without doubt, and carry a new wariness that will make him slower but wiser. The quality lost is real, but what remains is not ruin. It is a man edited, not emptied. Vesik poles back through the fog without comment. The letters arrive on time. The player's agent gains minor positive reputation with Diehl (he felt the god's steadying hand) and neutral standing with local travelers.

**Progress:** Diehl crosses. Letters delivered. Toll paid at minimum cost to selfhood.
**Spent:** Minor divine essence.
**New opening:** Diehl remembers the god's help. The crossing persists. Diehl's new wariness may make him a more interesting agent in future encounters.

**Success:**
The god steadies Diehl enough that he boards the punt, but the intervention is blunt. Diehl's fear does not settle; it is simply overridden. He crosses in a state of numb compliance. Vesik names the toll: "Your ease with strangers." Diehl pays it without fully understanding what he is agreeing to. When he reaches the far bank, he is functional but colder. He will deliver the letters. He will complete his duties. But he will not trust new faces as readily as he once did, and the warmth he carried into conversations with unfamiliar people is gone, replaced by a cautious distance that will never fully explain itself to him.

**Progress:** Diehl crosses. Letters delivered.
**Spent:** Minor divine essence.
**New opening:** Diehl is changed in a way he cannot articulate. The crossing persists. Minor.

**Success at Cost:**
The god pushes Diehl onto the punt, but the push is too forceful. Diehl boards feeling not steadied but compelled, and Vesik senses the divine pressure behind the courier's movement. The ferryman's eyes narrow. He names a steeper toll than he might have otherwise: "Your memory of being loved." Diehl pays it because he is already on the water and the fog has closed behind him. When he reaches the far bank, he stands for a moment as if expecting someone to be there. No one is. He thinks about this and feels nothing, which is new, and does not understand why the nothing has a shape. He delivers the letters. He does not know why he feels so cold.

**Progress:** Diehl crosses. Letters delivered at severe personal cost.
**Spent:** Minor divine essence. Diehl's capacity for felt love.
**New burden:** The ferryman noticed divine interference and may adjust future tolls. Diehl is functional but emotionally hollowed. The god's intervention made the transaction more expensive, not less.

**Failure:**
The god's steadying touch does not reach Diehl clearly enough. He feels a moment of warmth, then it passes. His fear reasserts itself. He steps back from the punt, shakes his head, and decides to take the four-day detour downstream. Vesik watches him go without expression. The letters arrive late. The garrison commander is displeased. Diehl is whole, but he has failed his errand on time.

**Progress:** None. Diehl does not cross. Letters delayed.
**Spent:** Minor divine essence.
**New burden:** Diehl's professional reputation suffers. The crossing stands unchallenged. Minor.

**Critical Failure:**
The god's intervention is felt by both Diehl and Vesik. Diehl surges forward onto the punt with divine-fueled confidence, but the confidence is brittle and borrowed. Vesik reads it instantly -- he has seen thirty years of travelers, and this one smells of outside influence. He names a toll that is almost punitive: "Your name." Not the word. The felt identity beneath it -- the sense of being *Diehl*, of knowing who you are when you wake in the dark. Diehl, flooded with false courage, agrees before he understands. When he reaches the far bank, he cannot remember who sent him or why. He wanders toward the garrison with a satchel of letters he cannot explain, a horse he left on the far side, and a void where his selfhood was. Vesik poles back through the fog, grim and satisfied in a way that disturbs even him. The god's meddling turned a manageable toll into a catastrophe.

**Progress:** Diehl crosses but is effectively destroyed as a person. Letters may or may not be delivered.
**Spent:** Minor divine essence. Diehl's identity.
**New burden:** The ferryman has been provoked into taking more than he otherwise would. The crossing is now more predatory. The god made it worse.

## 10. Sample Opening Paragraph

The fog sat on the river like a hand pressed over a mouth. It did not move. It did not thin toward the banks or gather in the hollows between the limestone ridges the way honest fog does; it simply occupied the crossing, grey-white and absolute, as if the air itself had opinions about who should pass. The punt was visible only as a shape -- flat-bottomed, dark-planked, riding low in the current with the stillness of something that had been waiting longer than wood should last. The man standing on it held a pole that was taller than he was, its end wrapped in river-weed that he had not cleaned because the weed was part of it now, part of him, part of the arrangement. He was thin in the way that fog-country people are thin, as if the damp had found its way into his joints and eaten whatever softness had once lived there. His name was Vesik. He had been the ferryman for thirty-one years, and in that time he had never once asked for coin. On the near bank, a young man in a courier's dustcoat stood beside a horse that would not stop pulling at its tether. The horse could smell the fog. The courier could smell it too -- river-silt and something older, something that made the teeth ache -- but he had letters to deliver and the ford was four days south, and so he stood at the waterline and listened to the ferryman explain, in a voice as flat and careful as a man reading terms he did not write, exactly what the crossing would cost.

## 11. Branch-Dependent Later Paragraphs

### Branch A: Break the Bargain

The god reached into the fog the way a hand reaches into still water -- slowly, and with the knowledge that whatever lives below will feel the disturbance. The contract was not visible. It was not a scroll or a chain or a mark on Vesik's skin. It was a pressure, a tautness in the space between the ferryman and the river, as if the current held one end of a rope and the man held the other and both had been pulling for so long that neither remembered a time before the tension. The god found the rope. The god cut it. Vesik made a sound that was not a word. He dropped the pole. It floated for a moment, trailing its river-weed like hair, and then the current took it and it was gone. The punt rocked. On the near bank, Diehl stepped back from the waterline, because the fog had moved for the first time in his memory -- not thinning, exactly, but shifting, as if the thing that held it in place had lost its grip. Vesik sat down heavily on the punt's wet planks and pressed his hands against his face, and when he lowered them his eyes were different. They were the eyes of a man who had just woken from a dream that lasted thirty-one years, and who could not yet tell whether waking was mercy or punishment.

### Branch B: Steady the Courier

The god leaned into the courier's fear the way wind leans into a candle -- not enough to extinguish, just enough to steady. Diehl felt it. He did not know what it was. He thought perhaps it was the memory of his father telling him that a man who stops walking has already decided to fail, or perhaps it was nothing more than the particular quality of light that sometimes breaks through fog and makes the world seem briefly navigable. He stepped onto the punt. The planks were wet and the wood was cold, and the punt sank a quarter-inch under his weight in a way that made him think of something settling into place. Vesik looked at him. The ferryman's fog-pale eyes moved across the courier's face the way a needle moves across cloth -- finding the weave, testing the tension -- and whatever he found there he accepted without expression. "Your certainty," Vesik said, "that you are doing the right thing." He said it the way a shopkeeper names a price -- without apology, without explanation, as a statement of what the transaction requires. Diehl opened his mouth to ask what that meant, and then closed it, because he already knew. He could feel it: the particular weight of conviction that had carried him through the last six days on the road, the private assurance that the letters mattered, that his errand was worth the saddle-ache and the cold mornings and the long miles. Vesik held out his hand. Diehl took it. And the ferryman poled them out into the fog, and the current took them, and the near bank disappeared behind them like a sentence left unfinished.

## 12. Aftermath Paragraph

### Branch A: Break the Bargain

By the following morning the fog had thinned enough that the limestone ridges were visible from both banks, and travelers who had been crossing at this point for years stood at the waterline and stared at the empty river as if something had died. The punt had lodged in a mudbank downstream, half-submerged, its wood already greying in the open air as if thirty-one years of service were catching up to it all at once. Vesik was sitting on the near bank with his knees drawn up, watching the water. He had not spoken since the contract broke. Diehl had stayed with him through the night, though he could not have said why, and when morning came the courier left his horse tethered and walked downstream to the ford, carrying his satchel on foot, because the letters still needed delivering and some obligations do not dissolve when the fog does. The river ran clear and cold and ordinary, and whatever had lived in the contract between Vesik and the current was gone, and the crossing was gone with it, and the travelers who had paid their soul-tolls over thirty-one years would not get them back, because the river does not make refunds.

### Branch B: Steady the Courier

Diehl reached the far bank as the fog was thickening into its afternoon density, and when he stepped off the punt he stumbled -- not from the motion of the water, but from the sudden lightness of a man who has set down a weight he did not know he was carrying and discovered he needed it. Vesik poled back without a word. The punt disappeared into the fog, and the sound of the pole dipping into the water faded, and Diehl stood on the muddy bank with his satchel of letters and tried to remember why he had been so sure they mattered. They probably did. He would deliver them. He would do his job. He would continue being Diehl in every way that could be measured or observed. But the private engine that had driven him -- the felt conviction that his work meant something, that carrying messages between people who needed to speak to each other was a form of service worth the road -- was quiet now, and in its place was a space that was not empty but was no longer warm. He walked toward the garrison. The horse was still on the other side. Someone would bring it around eventually. Behind him, the fog closed over the crossing like a mouth.

## 13. Aftermath Reaction Choices

No reaction choices -- consequence is clean. The two branches produce clearly divergent world states that do not require further player input to land. Branch A closes the crossing and frees Vesik; Branch B preserves the crossing and diminishes Diehl. Both consequences flow naturally into reputation channels and future encounter hooks without an additional selection step. The encounter's moral weight is already carried by the initial branch choice.

**Curated visible changes:**

*Branch A (Break the Bargain):*
- The Silt crossing is closed. No ferry service. Travelers must use the downstream ford (four-day detour).
- Vesik is unbound. Mark: "Unbound Ferryman" -- a man freed from a thirty-one-year contract, disoriented and purposeless.
- The fog at the crossing begins to thin over the following days.
- Diehl is whole. His letters are delivered late via the ford.

*Branch B (Steady the Courier):*
- The Silt crossing persists. Vesik continues to operate. The toll continues.
- Diehl carries the mark "Diminished" -- a named soul-quality is gone. The specific quality depends on outcome tier.
- Letters delivered on time. Garrison receives its correspondence.
- The crossing's reputation is unchanged; the small economy of diminishment continues.

**Notable marks/conditions:**
- Vesik: "Unbound" (Branch A) or unchanged (Branch B). On critical failure of Branch A, Vesik is deeper in the river's grip.
- Diehl: whole (Branch A) or "Diminished" (Branch B). On critical failure of Branch B, Diehl loses his identity entirely.
- The Silt crossing: closed (Branch A) or operational (Branch B). On critical failure of either branch, the crossing is more dangerous than before.

**What the world remembers:**
- Local travelers remember whether the ferryman's toll was broken or permitted to continue. This colors future encounters involving river crossings, liminal bargains, or divine interference in local economies.
- Vesik remembers whether a god freed him or left him in the fog. If freed, he is a potential future NPC -- a man searching for purpose after thirty-one years of mechanism.
- Diehl remembers the crossing differently depending on the branch: as the place where a god intervened and nothing happened to him (Branch A), or as the place where he paid something he cannot name and has not stopped missing (Branch B).
- The river remembers being tested (failure/critical failure of Branch A). This is not anthropomorphism; it is the mechanical truth that the contract resisted interference and may be harder to break in the future.

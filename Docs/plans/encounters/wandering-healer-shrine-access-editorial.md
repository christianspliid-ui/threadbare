# Encounter Pipeline: The Healer at the Ward-Gate
> Scale: short | Slug: wandering-healer-shrine-access | Pass: editorial
> Date: 2026-04-04 | Pipeline version: 1.0

---

## 1. Prose Quality

This is strong writing. The draft author has genuine command of scene construction, and across all three sample passages, the prose operates as fiction rather than as encounter documentation wearing a prose costume. That said, the quality is uneven -- the opening paragraph is excellent, the linear continuation is very good with one structural wobble, and the aftermath paragraph has a specific passage that underperforms the rest.

### Sample Opening Paragraph

The opening is the best passage in the draft. It earns its length. The sentence that begins "She was not dramatic about it" immediately establishes voice and point of view -- we are watching through a god's eye that notices *restraint*, not spectacle. The mule, the panniers, the dried comfrey -- these are working details, not set dressing. They tell us who Maret is before we learn her name.

The upward camera movement to the second-story window is cinematic and earns the transition from ground-level patience to aerial desperation. "The particular stillness of someone who has run out of things to try" is the paragraph's best phrase -- it does emotional work without telling the reader what to feel.

The closing sentence -- "bone-fever does not wait" -- lands cleanly as a ticking clock. The paragraph is a single unbroken block, which is the right choice: the scene is continuous, the tension is unbroken, and paragraph breaks would let the reader breathe when they should not.

One minor note: the sentence about Jorik's jaw ("arms folded, jaw set") is the weakest physical description in the paragraph. It reads as stock body language. But it is immediately rescued by the line about his mother, so the damage is minimal.

**Assessment: Meets the bar. No rewrite needed.**

### Linear Continuation Paragraph

The opening simile -- "the way wind leans into a flame -- not enough to extinguish, just enough to bend" -- is good. It captures the intervention fantasy (nudge, not smash) in a single image. The neighbor crossing the square is the right dramatic mechanism: change arrives through community, not divine command.

The passage where Jorik opens the gate is well-executed: "six inches open -- no wider" is a physical detail that carries enormous social weight. His line of dialogue ("The shrine garden. Moonwort. Nothing else. I walk with you.") is excellent -- clipped, controlled, and revealing character through compression. This is a man giving ground one inch at a time.

However, the sentence "Maret stood and gathered her satchel without haste, as if she had always known the door would open, and perhaps she had" weakens the scene. The "as if she had always known" construction is a narrator's commentary that steps on what should be a moment of quiet action. It tells us to admire Maret's composure rather than letting us see it. The sentence before it (Jorik's dialogue) and the sentence after it (the mother pressing her hand against the glass) are both showing. This one is telling.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE]
> Maret stood and gathered her satchel the way she gathered everything -- without haste, without thanks, as if haste and thanks were luxuries she had stopped carrying somewhere on the road between her fourth village and her fortieth. The mule stayed at the bench. She followed Jorik through the narrow gap in the gate and into the Thornwall Ward, and overhead the mother in the window pressed her hand against the glass and did not breathe.

The rewrite keeps Maret's composure but roots it in her character (a woman who has done this many times) rather than in narratorial mysticism ("perhaps she had"). It also maintains the paragraph's rhythm by matching the sentence length to its neighbors.

### Aftermath Paragraph

The first half is strong. "A grey powder she did not name" is excellent -- it preserves Maret's mystery and competence without exposition. The shrine-keeper watching "from a distance that was not hostile but was not warm" precisely captures the ward's social temperature.

The passage about Jorik closing the gate ("with more force than was necessary, and the bolt rang like a bell") does real work -- it shows us that the gate opened but the wound did not close.

The problem is the final sentence: "Jorik sat in his alcove and stared at the closed gate and thought about his mother, who had also been kind to strangers, and who had died for it, and who would have opened the gate three hours earlier than he did."

This sentence is doing too much. It is reaching for a resonant closing beat -- and the content is right (Jorik's mother as the ghost haunting his decision) -- but the execution is a list of three relative clauses hung off the same subject. The rhythm is monotonous: "who had also... and who had died... and who would have." The tricolon structure makes it feel like a eulogy being read aloud rather than a man's private thought. Jorik's interiority deserves better than grammatical symmetry.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE]
> In the ward, Jorik sat in his alcove and stared at the closed gate. His mother had been kind to strangers. His mother had died for it. She would have opened the gate three hours earlier than he did, and she would have been wrong to do it, and she would have saved the child anyway, and he missed her.

The rewrite breaks the tricolon into short declarative sentences that feel like actual thought -- the way grief arrives in fragments, not in balanced clauses. The addition of "and she would have been wrong to do it" sharpens the dilemma: Jorik is not simply regretful. He knows the rules exist for real reasons *and* he knows his mother would have broken them. The final "and he missed her" lands as an emotional beat that the original's grammatical elegance actually suppresses.

## 2. Branch Seduction Audit

Linearity is the right call for this encounter.

The branching templates document notes that short encounters "should usually use one compact branching grammar or stay mostly linear" and that branch count 1 "is fine when the encounter is stronger as a single sharp intervention." This encounter is precisely that case. The intervention fantasy is about *nudging* -- the player acts, and the world's response is graduated, not forked. A two-branch choice (e.g., "nudge gently" vs. "push hard") would flatten the outcome ladder by making the player telegraph their desired consequence tier. The current design makes the *world* decide how much it yields, which is more interesting and more thematically honest.

A Doctrine Split (mercy vs. respect-for-local-authority) was the obvious branching candidate, and the draft is right to reject it. The encounter already contains that tension in the outcome ladder: critical success resolves the tension, success at cost pays for mercy with someone else's authority, and failure validates the ward's autonomy. Forcing the player to choose a doctrine up front would make them a tactician rather than a god. The point is that gods act and the world interprets.

The one place where linearity costs something: the player cannot choose to *not* intervene. The encounter fires and the outcome ladder rolls. If the system supports a "withdraw" option (walk away, let local processes play out), that would be a meaningful addition -- not as a branch, but as a valid response to the encounter prompt. But that is a systems question, not an editorial one.

## 3. Branch Count Assessment

Branch count 0 is correct.

The outcome ladder (five tiers from critical success to critical failure) does the work that branching would do in a longer encounter. Each tier produces materially different consequences: Jorik's authority is intact, undermined, or reinforced; Maret gains or loses settlement access; the child recovers quickly or slowly; the ward's outsider policy softens or hardens. These are not cosmetic variations -- they change who matters and what the world remembers.

**Recommendation: KEEP LINEAR.**

## 4. Scale Discipline Check

The encounter is correctly sized as short. One beat, one intervention pass, no forced branching. The pressure knot is a single scene in a single location with two primary NPCs and one supporting group. The outcome ladder has five tiers, which is standard for any scale.

The prose is generous for a short encounter -- the three sample paragraphs total roughly 700 words -- but that generosity serves the atmospheric and relational purpose the scale justification identifies. This is world-texture prose, not structural bloat. The beat count (1) matches the scale guideline. The cast is lean. The aftermath is clean. No inflation detected.

## 5. Inspiration Anchor Honesty

The inspiration anchors are genuinely load-bearing.

- **Tonal Bible ("Cultural Mosaic with Internal Contradictions"):** This directly shaped the ward's characterization. The Thornwall Ward is not monolithically xenophobic -- the neighbor who speaks to Jorik proves there is internal disagreement. The lung sickness backstory gives the outsider ban a historical wound rather than a personality trait. This is not cosmetic citation.

- **Tonal Bible ("Wonder Layered Over Grief"):** Maret's arrival carrying "genuine skill and real compassion" into a community shaped by loss -- this tension is visible in the prose and the outcome ladder. The wonder (a healer who can save the child) is layered over the grief (the last time outsiders came through this gate, people died).

- **Thematic Pillars ("Compassion vs. Power"):** The central axis of the encounter. Every outcome tier navigates this tension differently. Critical success resolves it; success at cost reveals its price; failure shows compassion losing to institutional power.

- **Anti-Patterns (#8, #9, #10):** All three are genuinely avoided. Maret is not an exposition device. Jorik is not wrong. The healer has a fallback plan.

- **Event Archetypes ("Plague Wind"):** The historical plague shaped the ward-gate's existence, Jorik's personal history, and the community's institutional memory. Remove the Plague Wind anchor and the encounter loses its backstory engine.

The only anchor not consulted is the Dilemma Library, which is correctly noted as unnecessary for a linear encounter.

**Assessment: All cited anchors materially shaped the encounter. No cosmetic references detected.**

## 6. Aftermath Payoff

The aftermath lands as a felt consequence, not a log entry. The curated visible changes are actor-centered: Maret departs (her trajectory changes), Jorik's authority is intact or damaged (his social standing shifts), the child recovers or suffers (the human stakes resolve). The world remembers through specific channels (ward disposition, Maret's personal bond, family standing) rather than through generic reputation points.

The aftermath paragraph (section 12) is the strongest piece of evidence: Jorik closing the gate "with more force than was necessary" is a visible, felt consequence. The mother finding Maret at the crossroads market with dried apricots -- this is the kind of aftermath detail that makes a player remember an encounter weeks later. It is not a stat change; it is a human gesture that carries forward.

The one weakness in the aftermath kit (section 14) is that the "What the world remembers" bullet about the child's family ("carries a quiet gratitude or a quiet shame") is underspecified compared to the Jorik and Maret entries. The ward and the healer have concrete memory channels; the family's memory is described but not given a clear mechanical surface. This may be intentional (the family is supporting cast, not primary), but it creates a slight asymmetry in the aftermath's felt weight. This is a minor concern, not a blocker.

## 7. Dilemma Energy

For a linear encounter, the outcome ladder creates genuine tension. The spread between critical success ("the community's uneasy but genuine consent") and critical failure ("driven away from the gate with suspicion") is wide enough that the player feels real stakes. More importantly, the middle tiers are not just interpolations -- each has its own character:

- **Success** is competent and cold ("Jorik insists on watching her the entire time").
- **Success at Cost** is the most dramatically interesting tier: the child is saved, but Jorik's authority is broken, and "the cost of mercy was paid in someone else's authority." This is the tier that makes the encounter memorable. The mercy worked, and it cost something the player did not intend to spend.

The failure tiers are honest. The child survives eventually (bone-fever is slow), which avoids the anti-pattern of punishing the player with dead children for a failed roll. The critical failure escalates socially rather than medically -- Maret is driven from the whole settlement, the family is shamed -- which keeps the consequences in the encounter's proper register (social fabric, not body count).

The dilemma energy is real. The player is not choosing between "good outcome" and "bad outcome" -- they are acting, and the world is deciding how much it will bend.

## 8. Verdict

**PASS WITH REVISIONS**

This is a well-authored encounter that meets the quality bar for fiction, choice architecture, and player experience. The pressure knot is vivid, the intervention fantasy is clear and emotionally resonant, the outcome ladder creates genuine graduated tension, and the inspiration anchors are honestly used. The linear structure is the right call.

Two prose passages need the included rewrites: a telling-not-showing moment in the linear continuation, and a rhythmically monotonous closing sentence in the aftermath. Both rewrites are provided above. Neither changes the encounter's structure or meaning -- they sharpen prose that is already close to the bar.

Proceed to systems audit using the revised versions.

# Encounter Pipeline: The Broker's Ledger
> Scale: medium | Slug: rival-shrine-betrayal | Pass: editorial
> Date: 2026-04-04 | Pipeline version: 1.0

---

## 1. Prose Quality

This encounter is unusually well-written for a draft pass. The prose has a distinct voice, a controlled register, and -- critically -- a sensory specificity that most encounter drafts lack entirely. That said, there are passages where the writing overperforms in service of atmosphere and underperforms in service of dramatic motion.

### Opening Paragraph (Section 10)

Strong. Tessaly's introduction is economically drawn: the ledger-as-surgical-surface, the precise volume calibration, the red ink bleeding through. The caravanserai noise is good set-dressing that earns its keep by establishing Tessaly's professional control. The dialogue is sharp and reveals character through behavior rather than description. "Whoever buys it will not conduct business at a table" is the kind of line that does triple duty -- threat, characterization, and worldbuilding.

One weakness: the smith "hammering something too thin to be useful on a flat stone near the door" is overspecific background detail that pulls focus without payoff. It reads like the author proving they can see the room rather than letting the reader see it.

### Branch A -- Accept Path (Section 11)

This is the encounter's strongest prose passage. The sensory density of the curing sheds -- the calcium-ite vein, the tidal timing, Carin's hands moving like a musician's -- creates an intimacy that makes the betrayal land physically. The closing image of the seabirds and tidal pools flashing like mirrors while the agent records what will destroy the settlement's sovereignty is genuinely affecting. The passage earns its length.

One weakness: "The agent listened. The agent recorded." is a deliberate rhetorical choice (anaphora to mark the moral pivot), but it lands slightly flat because the sentence rhythm has already been steady and declarative for the preceding paragraph. The turn needs more friction. The reader should feel the shift from observation to espionage, and right now the prose glides over it.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE -- Accept path pivot]

Original: "The agent listened. The agent recorded. The details accumulated in neat columns in a journal that would leave Brinewall before the next tide."

Rewrite: "The agent listened. And the agent recorded -- not the way a guest remembers a host's generosity, but the way a surveyor records a claim. The details accumulated in neat columns in a journal that would leave Brinewall before the next tide."

### Branch B -- Refuse Path (Section 11)

Also strong, though it works in a different register. The refusal scene's power comes from restraint: Tessaly's professionalism absorbing the blow, the fire metaphor for something going quiet inside her. The exit image -- exact change for the tea, the east door, the ordinary northern light -- is the kind of careful, understated closure that rewards attentive reading.

One weakness: the simile "the way a fire goes quiet when the last good log burns through and what remains is the settling of ash" is good but overextended. By the time we reach "the settling of ash," the sentence has spent its energy. The image should land harder and earlier.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE -- Refuse path fire simile]

Original: "Her voice was the same measured professional instrument it had been from the beginning, but something underneath it had gone quiet, the way a fire goes quiet when the last good log burns through and what remains is the settling of ash."

Rewrite: "Her voice was the same measured professional instrument it had been from the beginning, but something underneath it had gone quiet -- the way a fire goes quiet when the last good log burns through."

### Accept Path Aftermath (Section 12)

Effective. The parallelism between Brinewall's unchanged daily life and the Greywater warehouse is well-constructed dramatic irony. The closing line -- "The god had gained a weapon and spent a friendship" -- is clean and does the right thematic work.

No rewrites needed.

### Refuse Path Aftermath (Section 12)

This is the weakest prose passage in the encounter. Not bad, but noticeably flatter than the other three. The problem is structural: the passage needs to convey that nothing happened, and "nothing happened" is hard to make compelling. The current version leans on parallel construction (the fish came in, the tidal pools filled, the monopoly held) which creates a pleasant rhythmic surface but does not generate dramatic energy. The passage reads as competent summary rather than felt experience.

The closing thought -- "Whether that choice was wise depended on what the rival god did next" -- is the right idea but arrives too cleanly. The player just made a sacrifice whose value cannot be measured. The prose should sit with that uncertainty rather than packaging it as a tidy conditional.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE -- Refuse path aftermath, full replacement]

Original starts: "The shrine in the Vessen uplands continued to operate..."

Rewrite:

"The shrine in the Vessen uplands continued to grow in its hidden valley, fed by prayers the player's agents could not hear and sustained by a rival whose patience had not yet been tested. The intelligence gap remained -- not as an absence the player could see, but as a shape in the dark that other gods could navigate and the player could not.

In Brinewall, the fish came in and the fish went out. Carin Harken walked the curing sheds with her daughter, and the calcium-ite held, and the monopoly held, and the settlement's small sovereignty continued for reasons no one in the settlement would ever fully understand. A god had refused to sell them and they would never know it.

That was the nature of the kindness: invisible, unmeasured, and impossible to spend. Whether it was also wise was a question only the rival god's next move could answer."

## 2. Branch Seduction Audit

### Branch A -- Accept the Trade

**Fantasy of interference:** Divine realpolitik. The god as strategist who accepts collateral damage in service of a larger board position. This is the "information is power" fantasy, and it is well-served: the shrine location is genuinely valuable, the settlement's monopoly is genuinely finite, and the calculation is defensible without being comfortable.

**Why a god would choose this:** Because the rival shrine is an active, growing threat in contested territory, and intelligence advantages compound. A god who refuses every hard trade will lose to a god who doesn't.

**What value it protects:** Strategic position. Future agent safety. The player's ability to contest a rival god's expansion.

**Seduction strength:** High. The prose does critical work here -- the extraction scene at the curing sheds makes the player feel the cost in sensory detail, which paradoxically makes the choice more seductive because it feels weighty rather than cheap. The player who takes this path is choosing to look while they betray, not looking away. That is a powerful fantasy.

### Branch B -- Refuse the Trade

**Fantasy of interference:** Divine integrity. The god whose commitments are not fungible. This is the "my word means something" fantasy, and it works because the refusal has real tactical cost: the shrine stays hidden, the rival keeps operating, and Tessaly's desperation becomes a loose thread.

**Why a god would choose this:** Because trust is infrastructure and alliances built on expendability collapse under pressure. A principled reputation compounds across all future relationships.

**What value it protects:** The player's network integrity. The principle that allies are not currency. Brinewall's sovereignty.

**Seduction strength:** High, but slightly lower than Branch A for one specific reason: the refuse path's immediate reward (Brinewall's deepened trust) is modest and invisible to the settlement, while the accept path's immediate reward (the shrine location) is tangible and actionable. The draft acknowledges this asymmetry in the self-audit but does not fully correct for it in the prose. The refuse path needs its aftermath reaction choices to work harder to close the gap -- and they do (the intelligence-gathering arc and the Tessaly-monitoring arc are both satisfying follow-ons), so the asymmetry is acceptable but worth noting.

### Asymmetry Assessment

**Moral asymmetry:** Well-balanced. Neither path is obviously right. The accept path is defensible as pragmatism; the refuse path is defensible as principle. Both gods are recognizable as gods.

**Dramatic asymmetry:** Slight imbalance favoring the accept path. The extraction scene (sitting in the Harken home, recording secrets over tea) is a more vivid dramatic situation than the refusal scene (watching Tessaly close a ledger and leave). The editorial rewrite of the refuse aftermath helps, but the accept path has an inherent scenic advantage because active betrayal generates more dramatic texture than active restraint.

**Information asymmetry:** Well-designed. The accept path grants concrete intelligence; the refuse path preserves a relationship. These are genuinely different currencies and neither dominates the other.

**Prose asymmetry:** Nearly balanced after the editorial rewrite of the refuse aftermath. Before the rewrite, the accept path was notably stronger in prose quality.

**Aftermath asymmetry:** Well-balanced. Both paths seed meaningful future encounters. The accept path's follow-on hooks (shrine confrontation, Brinewall investigation, Guild competition) are more numerous, but the refuse path's hooks (Tessaly's escalation, independent intelligence work) are more tightly wound.

**Overall:** Both branches pass the seduction audit. No branch needs cutting or radical strengthening.

## 3. Branch Count Assessment

**Is 2 the right count?** Yes.

The draft's reasoning for cutting the third branch (a counter-offer that avoids the dilemma) is sound. A counter-offer branch would function as an escape hatch -- the player gets the shrine location without betraying Brinewall, collapsing the moral tension that makes the encounter worth playing. Any counter-offer valuable enough to satisfy Tessaly's debt would need its own cost structure, and that cost would inevitably feel abstract compared to the vivid, specific harm of betraying a named family in a named settlement. The encounter's power comes from the binary: spend an ally or keep a principle. A third path that splits the difference would dilute both.

The two branches are structurally distinct: different settings, different central cast members, different emotional registers, different aftermath consequences. They do not converge. This is correct for a Doctrine Split grammar -- the first choice establishes the moral frame, and everything downstream reflects that frame.

**Per-branch prose check:**
- Branch A (Accept): Full scene prose in Section 11. Complete.
- Branch B (Refuse): Full scene prose in Section 11. Complete.
- Branch A aftermath: Full paragraph in Section 12. Complete.
- Branch B aftermath: Full paragraph in Section 12 (with editorial rewrite recommended). Complete.

**Recommendation:** KEEP 2 BRANCHES.

## 4. Scale Discipline Check

**Declared scale:** Medium.

**Beat count:** 2 beats (The Offer, then either The Extraction or The Refusal and Its Wake). This is correct for medium. Two beats with a single choice point gives the encounter enough room to breathe without overstaying.

**Complexity:** The branching is light (two paths, no sub-branches, no reconvergence). The outcome ladder is five tiers per path, which is standard. The aftermath reaction choices are two per path, which is restrained. All appropriate for medium.

**Consequence weight:** Consequential but localized. The encounter affects one settlement's economy, one broker's survival, one intelligence gap, and one alliance relationship. It does not reshape the regional power balance or trigger faction-level conflict directly, though its follow-on hooks could. This is the right weight for medium -- heavy enough that the choice matters, light enough that the player is not making a world-historical decision every time they encounter a broker in a caravanserai.

**Verdict:** The encounter fits medium correctly. No scale change needed.

## 5. Inspiration Anchor Honesty

The anchors are not decorative. The draft demonstrates material influence from each source:

- **Tonal Bible:** "Deep Time, Revealed in Fragments" genuinely shaped the information structure -- Tessaly's intelligence is assembled from fragments over seventeen years, not received as a complete package. "Favor as Hunger" shaped the transactional frame -- the broker's offer is leverage that curdles.

- **Thematic Pillars:** "Compassion vs. Power" is the encounter's primary axis and it shows up in the actual choice structure, not just the framing. "Sovereignty vs. Consumption" is the most interesting anchor citation -- the trade secret is framed explicitly as the settlement's economic sovereignty, and surrendering it converts them from self-sufficient to dependent. This reframing elevates the encounter beyond a simple information-trade puzzle.

- **Anti-Patterns:** All five cited anti-patterns are demonstrably avoided. The broker has her own Maslow-driven need. The settlement is not helpless. The technique is geographically specific. The moral binary is genuinely ambiguous.

- **Dilemma Library:** The draft claims the library "materially changed the choice set," and this is credible. Without the Honesty vs. Cunning axis framing, the encounter would likely have defaulted to a tactical optimization puzzle. The library pushed the choices toward revealing divine character.

- **Event Archetypes:** Discovery and Betrayal Revealed both visibly shape the encounter's structure.

**Verdict:** Anchors are honest and materially influential. No concerns.

## 6. Aftermath Payoff

### Accept Path Aftermath

The aftermath lands well. The visible changes are curated and actor-centered:
- The map arrives (concrete, tactile reward)
- Brinewall's daily life continues unchanged (dramatic irony)
- The Greywater warehouse is already working (ticking clock)
- The closing line frames the transaction as arithmetic whose balance depends on character

The aftermath reaction choices are well-differentiated: "prepare Brinewall" is guilt-management that preserves the relationship, while "spend the intelligence" is doubling down on the strategic posture. Both are defensible and both generate distinct future threads.

### Refuse Path Aftermath

After the editorial rewrite, this aftermath lands adequately. The original was the encounter's weakest passage; the rewrite gives the invisible sacrifice more weight and the closing question more uncertainty.

The refuse path's aftermath reaction choices are strong: "find the shrine another way" commits resources to overcoming the tactical disadvantage cleanly, while "watch Tessaly" converts the refusal into active defense. Both feel like moves a god would make rather than cleanup tasks.

### Aftermath Kit Summary

The visible changes tables (Section 14) are well-constructed. Each entry is specific, actor-centered, and narratively consequential. The "hidden mark" and "principle established" entries are particularly good -- they represent consequences that will surface in future encounters without requiring immediate resolution.

**Verdict:** Aftermath payoff is strong on both paths. The refuse path needed the editorial rewrite but is satisfactory with it.

## 7. Dilemma Energy

This encounter has genuine dilemma energy. The choices create real tension because:

1. **Both paths have concrete, distinct costs.** The accept path costs an alliance and carries a ticking betrayal. The refuse path costs intelligence and leaves a rival god operating invisibly. Neither cost is abstract.

2. **Both paths reveal divine posture.** The accept path reveals a strategic god who spends allies for advantage. The refuse path reveals a principled god who accepts tactical disadvantage to preserve trust. These are recognizable, defensible, and non-trivially different postures of godhood.

3. **Neither path is obviously correct.** The draft correctly avoids clean moral binaries. The accept path is not evil -- the settlement's monopoly is genuinely finite, the shrine is a real threat, and the information has strategic value. The refuse path is not naive -- trust compounds, alliances built on expendability collapse, and the shrine can be found by other means.

4. **The costs are asymmetric in kind, not in quantity.** The accept path trades a relationship for intelligence. The refuse path trades intelligence for a relationship. These are different currencies, and the player's valuation reveals their character.

5. **The prose supports the tension.** The extraction scene makes the betrayal visceral (eating plums in the curing shed while recording the family's secrets). The refusal scene makes the restraint dignified (watching Tessaly close her ledger with professional calm while her future collapses). Neither scene is moralized; both let the player sit with what they've chosen.

**One concern:** The pressure knot's framing that the technique has "perhaps five to ten years before someone reverse-engineers it anyway" slightly undermines the refuse path by suggesting the betrayal is inevitable regardless. This is realistic worldbuilding, but it risks making the refuse path feel like delay rather than prevention. The refuse path aftermath (as rewritten) handles this correctly by framing the invisible sacrifice as valuable in itself, but the pressure knot's language tips the scales slightly toward "accept" by suggesting the technique's secrecy is a depreciating asset. This is a minor issue and does not require structural changes, but the systems agent should be aware that the encounter's framing slightly favors the pragmatic reading.

## 8. Verdict

**PASS WITH REVISIONS**

This is a strong encounter draft. The prose is above the quality bar, the dilemma is genuine, the branches are well-differentiated, the scale is correct, and the inspiration anchors are honest. The encounter does what a medium encounter should do: it presents a meaningful choice with lasting consequences, gives the player room to feel the weight of the decision, and seeds future encounter threads that reflect the player's divine character.

The required revisions are minor prose edits that strengthen the weaker passages without changing structure.

## 9. Revision Summary

### Must fix
- **Refuse path aftermath (Section 12):** Replace the flat original with the editorial rewrite. The current passage is noticeably weaker than the other three prose sections and fails to convey the weight of the invisible sacrifice.

### Should fix
- **Accept path pivot (Section 11):** Replace the anaphora transition with the editorial rewrite. The current version glides over the moral shift; the rewrite adds the friction the moment needs.
- **Refuse path fire simile (Section 11):** Trim the overextended simile per the editorial rewrite. The image loses force by the time it reaches "the settling of ash."

### Consider
- **Opening paragraph smith detail:** The smith hammering something too thin to be useful is vivid but pulls focus. Consider cutting "on a flat stone near the door" to reduce the specificity of a background detail that serves no narrative function.
- **Pressure knot depreciation framing:** The "five to ten years" language slightly favors the accept path by framing the secret as a depreciating asset. Not a structural problem, but worth awareness.

# Encounter Pipeline: The Broker's Ledger

> Scale: medium | Slug: rival-shrine-betrayal | Pass: revised
> Date: 2026-04-04 | Pipeline version: 1.0

---

## 1. Inspiration Anchors

**Foundation references:**

- **Tonal Bible** -- "Deep Time, Revealed in Fragments" shaped how the rival shrine's location enters the encounter. The broker does not have a map. She has a fragment of an old surveyor's journal, a line from a dead cartographer's margin notes, and a pattern she has pieced together from seventeen years of trading information between factions. The shrine's location is discovered knowledge, not given knowledge. The Tonal Bible's "Favor as Hunger" also shaped the entire transaction: the broker is offering the player exactly the kind of leverage that curdles -- knowledge purchased with someone else's livelihood. Divine power exercised through information brokerage is power that looks clean and costs someone else everything.
- **Thematic Pillars** -- "Compassion vs. Power" is the primary axis: the player can gain strategic advantage over a rival god at the cost of a settlement's economic survival. "Order vs. Freedom" is the secondary axis: the trade secret the broker wants is a collectively held community process (a salt-curing technique that makes their fish the only reliably preserved food source on the northern coast), and surrendering it dissolves the structural advantage that lets a small settlement resist absorption by larger powers. "Legacy vs. Reinvention" shapes the aftermath: if the secret is betrayed, the settlement must reinvent itself or die. The "Sovereignty vs. Consumption" canonical reframing is deeply relevant -- this encounter is a moment where the player-god can choose to consume an ally's sovereignty for personal gain. The trade secret is the settlement's economic sovereignty; surrendering it turns them from self-sufficient to dependent.
- **Anti-Patterns** -- Actively avoiding #9 (Clean Moral Binaries): the broker is not a villain. She is a professional who trades in information the way others trade in grain. She does not enjoy the cost of this particular trade; she simply does not weight it against the value. Avoiding #8 (Helpful Exposition NPC): the broker has her own Maslow-driven need -- she wants the trade secret because she has a buyer in a rival faction who will pay enough to settle a debt that has been eating her life for three years. This is not altruistic information exchange; it is desperation wearing a professional face. Avoiding #10 (Player as Savior): the settlement has survived without the player's protection for decades. The trade secret keeps them alive, but they are not helpless -- they are resourceful, stubborn, and politically aware. What the player decides here changes the terms of their survival, not the fact of it. Avoiding #7 (Generic Fantasy Aesthetics): the settlement's salt-curing technique is specific, physical, and tied to their geography. It is not a generic "trade advantage" but a process involving particular tidal pools, a local mineral deposit, and a timing technique passed down through three generations of the same family.
- **Event Archetypes -- "Discovery (Knowledge Reframes Everything)"** and **"Betrayal Revealed (Trust Shattered)"** -- The Discovery archetype shaped the encounter's information structure: the rival shrine's location is a hidden truth that changes the regional power balance. The Betrayal Revealed archetype shaped the aftermath: if the player trades the secret, the settlement will eventually discover the betrayal, and the consequences are social and reputational, not just economic. The Discovery archetype's note that "revealing truth is treason" is particularly apt -- the trade secret is knowledge the settlement considers sacred, and exposing it to a faction broker is an act of betrayal even if no law forbids it.

**Dilemma Library consultation:**

The Dilemma Library materially changed this encounter's choice set. The **Honesty vs. Cunning** axis (Shadow reach) is the encounter's core moral grammar: the player must decide whether to traffic in secrets, and whether information brokerage is a legitimate tool of divine statecraft or a corruption of the trust their agents carry. The **Loyalty vs. Ambition** axis (Heart reach) is the secondary tension: the settlement is an ally whose livelihood the player can sacrifice for strategic gain against a rival god. The library's canonical framing -- that choices must "reveal what kind of god the player is willing to be" -- forced me to ensure both branches feel like genuine postures of godhood rather than tactical optimizations. The library's insistence that dilemmas "establish the first pressure points where care, usefulness, claim, and predation can blur together" directly shaped the encounter's central tension: the broker is offering the player a chance to be useful to themselves in a way that preys on an ally.

Without the Dilemma Library, this encounter would have defaulted to a simple information-trade puzzle. The library pushed the choice into revealing divine character: a god who trades allies' secrets is a different kind of god than one who refuses, and neither is straightforwardly right or wrong.

**Anti-patterns actively avoided:**
- Clean Moral Binaries (the broker is not evil; the settlement is not helpless; both choices have real costs)
- Helpful Exposition NPC (the broker has her own desperate need driving this transaction)
- Player as Savior (the settlement survives either way, on different terms)
- Generic Fantasy Aesthetics (the salt-curing technique is geographically specific and culturally embedded)
- Grimdark for Shock Value (the betrayal is structural and economic, not violent or gratuitous)

## 2. Scale Justification

This is a **medium** encounter because the stakes are consequential but localized. The encounter involves a genuine moral choice with lasting reputational and economic consequences, a named broker with her own arc, a settlement whose future is materially altered by the outcome, and intelligence about a rival god's hidden infrastructure. That is too much weight for a short encounter -- the choice deserves breathing room and the aftermath deserves a curated landing. But it is not a world-reshaping crisis. The rival shrine is one piece of a larger puzzle, the settlement is one community among many, and the broker is a professional intermediary, not a faction leader. Two beats with selective branching, a meaningful aftermath, and a follow-on hook: correctly sized for medium.

## 3. Pressure Knot

A faction broker named Tessaly has been working the northern trade routes for seventeen years, buying and selling information the way other merchants buy and selling wool. She is good at her work. She is not cruel and she is not kind. She is broke.

Three years ago she borrowed against a future intelligence sale that fell through when her source was killed in a border skirmish. The debt has been compounding. The faction she owes -- a mercantile consortium called the Saltern Compact -- has made it clear that her next payment is her last chance before they sell her debt to collectors who operate with less patience and more knives.

Tessaly has found a buyer for one specific piece of intelligence: the salt-curing technique used by the coastal settlement of Brinewall. The Saltern Compact's rival, a larger trading house called the Greywater Guild, will pay enough to clear Tessaly's debt twice over if she can deliver the technique. Brinewall's curing process -- which involves specific tidal pools, a local calcium-ite deposit, and a timing method developed over three generations by the Harken family -- is the only reason their preserved fish commands premium prices across the northern coast. Without that monopoly, Brinewall is just another fishing village. With it, they are economically sovereign: too valuable to absorb, too useful to threaten.

The player's agent has a relationship with Brinewall. The settlement has been an ally -- not a vassal, not a worshiper, but a community that has benefited from the player's influence and returned that benefit through trade, information, and occasional shelter for the player's other agents. The relationship is real but not deep enough to be called devotion.

Tessaly has approached the player's agent because she has something the player wants: the location of a rival god's hidden shrine. She has pieced it together from seventeen years of trade route intelligence -- margin notes, dead cartographers' journals, inconsistencies in pilgrimage routes. The shrine is real. The information is accurate. And Tessaly will trade it for the Brinewall salt-curing technique, which she knows the player's agent can access because Brinewall trusts them.

The rival god's shrine is not an immediate military threat. It is a node of worship, influence, and agent-seeding that the rival has been growing quietly in contested territory. Finding it would give the player a significant intelligence advantage: they could disrupt the rival's influence network, redirect their own agents to contest the territory, or simply know where the rival is investing power. Leaving it undiscovered means the rival continues to operate invisibly in a region the player cares about.

The world is already moving. Tessaly's debt comes due in eight days. If she cannot make this trade, she will try to steal the technique through other means -- messier, less reliable, but she is desperate enough. The Greywater Guild already suspects Brinewall has something worth knowing; Tessaly's approach simply offers a clean path to what others will eventually pursue by dirtier methods. Brinewall is not in immediate danger, but their secret has a shelf life measured in years, not decades. The question is not whether the secret will eventually spread, but who decides when, and what the settlement gets in return.

## 4. Intervention Fantasy

The player is a god who has just been offered a devil's bargain by someone who is neither devil nor saint. The fantasy is not heroic rescue or villainous scheming -- it is *statecraft*. The player is standing at a junction where information, loyalty, and power intersect, and they must decide what kind of god they are when nobody is watching and the cost falls on someone else.

The compelling part is the asymmetry of knowledge. The player knows what Tessaly is offering is valuable. They know what she is asking will hurt an ally. They know the ally will not find out immediately, and may never trace the betrayal back to divine influence. This is not a test of courage or strength. It is a test of character conducted in private, with real strategic consequences on both sides.

The intervention fantasy splits cleanly:
- **The god who trades:** You are a strategic deity. Allies are assets, and assets have value that can be spent. Brinewall will survive -- diminished, but surviving. The rival shrine's location is worth more than one settlement's monopoly. You are playing a long game and you are honest with yourself about the costs.
- **The god who refuses:** You are a god whose word means something even when breaking it would be profitable. Brinewall trusted your agent. That trust is not currency. The rival shrine can be found by other means -- slower, harder, but without the taste of betrayal. You accept a tactical disadvantage to preserve something you consider more important than tactics.

## 5. Cast and World Objects

**Primary NPCs:**
- **Tessaly** -- faction broker, early forties, wiry, sharp-featured. Seventeen years on the northern trade routes. Currently three years into a debt spiral with the Saltern Compact. Professional, not malicious. Carries a reputation for reliable information and fair dealing -- this particular trade is the first time she has asked a source to betray a community relationship, and she is not comfortable with it, but she is more uncomfortable with the alternative.
- **Elder Carin Harken** -- matriarch of the Harken family, keepers of Brinewall's salt-curing technique. Late sixties. The technique was developed by her grandmother, refined by her mother, and is currently practiced by Carin and her two adult children. Carin is not naive about the technique's value -- she knows other factions want it, and she has spent decades deflecting inquiries, bribes, and one attempted kidnapping. She will not appear directly in the encounter's opening beats but is central to the aftermath.

**Supporting NPCs / witnesses:**
- **The player's agent** -- the intermediary through whom the player acts. Their existing relationship with Brinewall is the lever Tessaly is trying to pull.
- **Tessaly's contact in the Greywater Guild** -- unnamed, offstage. The buyer for the technique. Their existence creates the economic pressure that makes the trade real.

**Involved factions or communities:**
- **Brinewall** -- coastal fishing settlement, population roughly 200. Economically sovereign through the salt-curing monopoly. Allied with the player in a low-key, mutually beneficial relationship. Not a vassal or worshiping community -- a practical partner.
- **The Saltern Compact** -- mercantile consortium. Tessaly's creditor. Regional power in commodity trading. Not directly involved in the encounter but their debt pressure is the engine driving Tessaly.
- **The Greywater Guild** -- larger trading house, rival to the Saltern Compact. The buyer for Brinewall's technique. Their acquisition of the technique would let them replicate the curing process at industrial scale, undercutting Brinewall's prices within two trading seasons.
- **The rival god's network** -- the hidden shrine belongs to a rival deity who has been seeding influence in contested territory. The shrine's discovery would reveal the rival's investment pattern and agent network in the region.

**Required places and sublocations:**
- **A neutral meeting point** -- a caravanserai, waystation, or market hall where Tessaly conducts business. Not Brinewall itself; the negotiation happens at a distance from the settlement it concerns.
- **Brinewall** -- referenced in aftermath. The tidal pools, the calcium-ite deposit, the Harken family's curing sheds.
- **The rival shrine** -- referenced in aftermath and follow-on hooks. Its location is the prize Tessaly offers.

**Required reward objects, burdens, and attachments:**
- **Reward (if trade is accepted):** Intelligence attachment -- the rival shrine's location, delivered as a map fragment and a set of route notes. Persistent, referenceable by future encounters and agent decisions.
- **Reward (if trade is refused):** Reputation attachment -- Brinewall's deepened trust. The settlement does not know the specifics of what the player refused, but the agent's continued reliability reinforces the relationship. Modest but real.
- **Burden (if trade is accepted):** Betrayal mark -- a hidden guilt marker. Brinewall does not know yet. But the Greywater Guild's replication of the technique will eventually be traceable, and when Brinewall investigates, the player's agent is on a short list of people who had access. This is a ticking social bomb.
- **Burden (if trade is refused):** Tessaly's desperation escalates. She will attempt to obtain the technique through other, messier means. The player has not solved the problem; they have refused to be the tool through which it is solved. A follow-on encounter seed.

**Required reputation or social-state channels:**
- **Brinewall community trust** -- the existing relationship channel. Affected by both outcomes.
- **Faction broker network reputation** -- how the player is perceived by information traders. Accepting the trade makes the player a reliable partner for future intelligence deals. Refusing makes them principled but less useful.
- **Rival god awareness** -- if the shrine location is obtained, the player gains an intelligence advantage that future encounters and agent decisions can reference.

## 6. Beat Structure

### Beat 1: The Offer

Tessaly presents the trade to the player's agent at a neutral location. She lays out both halves: what she has (the rival shrine's location) and what she wants (the Brinewall salt-curing technique). She is professional but not cold -- she acknowledges that the ask is uncomfortable. She does not pretend the settlement will not be harmed. She frames it as a calculation: the technique's monopoly has perhaps five to ten years left before someone reverse-engineers it anyway; trading it now, on the player's terms, at least purchases something of immediate strategic value.

The player receives the full picture: the value of the shrine location, the cost to Brinewall, Tessaly's personal stakes, and the broader economic context. This is the decision beat.

**Player choice point:** Accept the trade, or refuse it.

### Beat 2a (Accept path): The Extraction

The player's agent must actually obtain the technique from Brinewall. This is not a simple handoff -- the Harken family does not write the process down. It lives in practice, in the timing of the tides, in knowing which pools to use in which season. The agent must spend time in Brinewall, observe the process, and compile it into something Tessaly can deliver. This beat is about the texture of the betrayal: the agent sits with the Harken family, eats their food, watches Carin's hands work, and records what they see while the family trusts them completely.

The resolution roll happens here: can the agent extract a usable description of the technique without arousing suspicion?

### Beat 2b (Refuse path): The Refusal and Its Wake

The player's agent tells Tessaly no. The beat is about what happens in the silence after the refusal. Tessaly does not rage or threaten. She nods, closes her ledger, and begins calculating her remaining options. The player watches a professional accept a setback that may cost her everything.

The resolution roll happens here: does the agent's refusal also manage to redirect Tessaly away from pursuing the technique through other means? Can they offer something else, or at least convince her that Brinewall is too well-guarded to approach directly? Or does the refusal simply end the player's involvement while the problem continues?

## 7. Branching Profile

- **Branch depth:** `light`
- **Branch count:** `2`
- **Where branching lives:**
  - `scene prose` (beat 2 is entirely different depending on the choice)
  - `cast emphasis` (accept path centers the Harken family; refuse path centers Tessaly)
  - `outcome ladder` (different stakes being resolved)
  - `aftermath` (radically different world consequences)
  - `follow-on hooks` (different future encounter seeds)
- **Convergence policy:** `stays divergent through aftermath` -- the two paths do not reconverge. The world remembers different things depending on which path was taken.
- **Primary branching template:** **Doctrine Split** -- the choice at beat 1 establishes the moral grammar of the entire encounter. The player declares whether they are a god who spends allies or one who protects them, and everything that follows is shaped by that declaration.
- **Secondary branching template:** **Reveal / Contain / Displace** -- the trade secret is a piece of hidden knowledge. The accept path reveals it (to Tessaly and then the Greywater Guild). The refuse path attempts to contain it (keeping the technique within Brinewall). A hypothetical third option would displace it (move the secret somewhere else, change its form), but this encounter does not support that branch with enough weight to justify it.

**Why two branches, not three:**

I tested a third branch -- "counter-offer: give Tessaly something else of value to get the shrine location without betraying Brinewall." This branch failed the seduction audit. It reads as the obviously correct move: you get everything and sacrifice nothing of consequence. It collapses the moral tension by introducing an escape hatch. In practice, any counter-offer valuable enough to satisfy Tessaly's debt would itself carry costs, but those costs would be abstract and distant compared to the vivid, specific harm of betraying Brinewall. The third branch would drain dramatic energy from the two branches that actually work. Two strong branches that reveal divine character are better than three where one lets the player dodge the question.

## 8. Branching Map

### Accept path: Beat 1 choice -> Beat 2a

**Step 1 (The Offer) -> Step 2a (The Extraction):**
- Scene prose shifts from negotiation at a neutral site to intimate domestic observation at Brinewall
- Central cast shifts from Tessaly to the Harken family
- Emotional register shifts from professional calculation to quiet guilt
- The player experiences the cost of their choice in sensory detail: the smell of the curing sheds, the trust in Carin's voice as she explains a step of the process to her daughter while the agent watches and records

**Step 2a -> Aftermath (Accept):**
- The technique is delivered to Tessaly, who delivers it to the Greywater Guild
- The player receives the rival shrine's location as an intelligence attachment
- Brinewall's economic monopoly begins eroding within two trading seasons
- The betrayal mark is placed: when Brinewall eventually traces the leak, the player's agent is on the short list
- Tessaly's debt is cleared; she becomes a potential recurring contact for future intelligence trades
- Follow-on hooks: the rival shrine can now be contested; Brinewall's investigation into the leak becomes a future encounter; the Greywater Guild's industrial-scale curing operation changes regional trade dynamics

### Refuse path: Beat 1 choice -> Beat 2b

**Step 1 (The Offer) -> Step 2b (The Refusal and Its Wake):**
- Scene prose stays at the neutral meeting point but shifts from negotiation to aftermath-of-negotiation
- Central cast stays on Tessaly, but the emotional register shifts from professional to desperate
- The player experiences the cost of their choice as watching someone else's crisis continue without resolution
- The agent may attempt to redirect Tessaly or offer partial help, but the core fact is that the shrine location remains unknown

**Step 2b -> Aftermath (Refuse):**
- The player does not receive the rival shrine's location
- Brinewall's trust in the player's agent is preserved (and slightly deepened, though they do not know the specifics)
- Tessaly's debt crisis continues; she will pursue the technique through other channels, creating a future pressure thread
- The rival god's shrine remains hidden and operational
- Follow-on hooks: Tessaly's next attempt to obtain the technique (messier, possibly involving theft or bribery of a Harken family member); the rival shrine continues to grow in influence; the player must find the shrine through other intelligence means

## 9. Outcome Ladder

### Accept Path Outcomes

**Critical success:** The agent extracts a complete, accurate description of the technique without arousing any suspicion. Tessaly's information about the shrine location includes additional details she held back as insurance -- the shrine's guardian, the timing of its rituals, and the identity of the rival god's primary mortal agent in the region. The betrayal mark is still placed, but the trail is cold; Brinewall's eventual investigation will take longer to reach the player's agent.
- **Progress:** Full intelligence on the rival shrine network. Strategic advantage in contested territory.
- **Cost:** Brinewall's monopoly will collapse within two seasons. The betrayal exists even if it is not yet discovered.
- **New opening:** The rival shrine can be directly contested. Tessaly becomes a reliable intelligence contact.

**Success:** The agent extracts a usable description of the technique. Tessaly delivers the shrine location as promised -- accurate but without the bonus details. The extraction went smoothly but not invisibly; one of the Harken family members noticed the agent was unusually attentive, though they attributed it to genuine interest rather than espionage.
- **Progress:** Shrine location obtained. Solid intelligence advantage.
- **Cost:** Brinewall's monopoly collapses. The trail is warm; the suspicious family member will remember the agent's interest when the investigation begins.
- **New burden:** The betrayal mark is active and traceable.

**Success at cost:** The agent extracts a partial description -- enough for the Greywater Guild to begin replication, but with gaps that will slow their process by a year or more. Tessaly accepts it but delivers the shrine location with less detail than promised, claiming this is proportional. The extraction was noticeable; the Harken family is unsettled by the agent's behavior and has begun asking quiet questions.
- **Progress:** Partial shrine intelligence. Some strategic value.
- **Cost:** Brinewall's monopoly erodes more slowly but the betrayal is more visible. The Harken family's suspicion creates immediate social friction.
- **New burden:** Both the betrayal mark and an active suspicion thread in Brinewall.

**Failure:** The agent's extraction attempt is clumsy or incomplete. The technique description is too garbled to be useful. Tessaly refuses to trade the shrine location for unusable intelligence. The agent has spent time in Brinewall under false pretenses and the Harken family's trust is damaged even though the technique was not successfully stolen.
- **Progress:** None. No shrine location.
- **Cost:** Brinewall's trust is damaged. The agent is no longer welcome in the curing sheds.
- **New burden:** Damaged relationship with Brinewall. Tessaly's debt crisis continues. The player has paid a social cost for nothing.

**Critical failure:** The agent's espionage is discovered by the Harken family during the extraction. Carin Harken confronts the agent directly. The betrayal is exposed, public, and attributed to the player's influence. Brinewall severs the alliance. Tessaly, realizing her intermediary has been burned, cuts contact and seeks the technique through other channels that will be hostile to the player.
- **Progress:** None.
- **Cost:** Alliance with Brinewall destroyed. Reputation in the region damaged. Tessaly becomes an adversary rather than a contact.
- **New burden:** Active hostility from Brinewall. Regional reputation damage. Tessaly's future attempts to obtain the technique may now be motivated partly by spite.

### Refuse Path Outcomes

**Critical success:** The agent's refusal is delivered with enough diplomatic skill that Tessaly is not only convinced to abandon the Brinewall approach but also volunteers a partial lead on the rival shrine's location -- not the full coordinates, but enough to narrow the search significantly. She does this not out of generosity but because maintaining the player's goodwill is more valuable to her long-term than burning the relationship over one failed deal.
- **Progress:** Partial shrine intelligence obtained without betrayal. Brinewall relationship strengthened.
- **Cost:** The shrine location is incomplete; further intelligence work required.
- **New opening:** Tessaly remains a potential contact. Brinewall's trust deepens.

**Success:** The agent refuses cleanly and Tessaly accepts it professionally. The relationship with Tessaly is neutral -- not warm, but not hostile. Brinewall's trust is preserved. No shrine intelligence is obtained.
- **Progress:** Alliance with Brinewall preserved and slightly strengthened.
- **Cost:** No intelligence on the rival shrine. The rival continues to operate invisibly.
- **New opening:** The player can pursue the shrine through other means. Brinewall may offer future assistance.

**Success at cost:** The agent refuses but handles the conversation poorly -- either moralizing in a way that alienates Tessaly, or hesitating long enough that Tessaly suspects the player was tempted and may reconsider. Tessaly leaves the meeting with a grudge and a sense that the player's agent knows enough about Brinewall's technique to be a useful target for future pressure.
- **Progress:** Alliance with Brinewall preserved.
- **Cost:** Tessaly is now a minor adversary who may approach the player again with more leverage, or may target the player's agents for information through less pleasant means.
- **New burden:** An active adversary in the intelligence network.

**Failure:** The agent's refusal is so poorly handled that Tessaly interprets it as a counter-play -- she believes the player is trying to cut her out and obtain the shrine location through other channels without paying. She accelerates her timeline and begins pursuing the technique through direct theft from Brinewall, using the player's agent's known access to Brinewall as a diversionary cover story.
- **Progress:** None.
- **Cost:** Tessaly is actively hostile and targeting Brinewall. The player's agent may be blamed for any subsequent breach.
- **New burden:** An active threat to Brinewall that the player inadvertently enabled.

**Critical failure:** The refusal conversation goes catastrophically wrong. Tessaly leaves believing the player has decided to weaponize the information about her debt against her -- using her desperation as leverage. She takes preemptive action: she sells what she knows about the player's agent network to the rival god's faction, including the player's relationship with Brinewall. The rival god now knows the player has an unprotected ally.
- **Progress:** None.
- **Cost:** The rival god gains intelligence about the player's network. Brinewall becomes a target not because of the technique but because of its value to the player.
- **New burden:** The rival god's awareness of the player's agent network. Brinewall is newly visible to hostile forces.

## 10. Sample Opening Paragraph

Tessaly spread the ledger on the table between them and pressed it flat with both palms, the way a surgeon prepares a surface. The caravanserai was loud with the ordinary commerce of the northern routes -- drovers arguing about feed prices, a smith hammering something too thin to be useful on a flat stone near the door -- but the noise stopped at the edges of her voice the way water stops at wax. She did not whisper. She spoke at exactly the volume that carried no further than the table.

"I know where the Pale Court keeps its shrine in the Vessen uplands. I have known for two months. I have not sold this to anyone because I wanted to sell it to you, and because what I want in return is something only your people can give me." She turned the ledger to face the agent. The page showed a column of debts in Tessaly's precise hand, each one crossed through except the last, which was circled in red ink that had bled through to the page beneath. "The Saltern Compact owns me for another eight days. After that, they sell the debt, and whoever buys it will not conduct business at a table."

She closed the ledger. Her fingers were steady but her knuckles were pale.

"Brinewall. The curing technique. The Harken family's process -- the tidal pools, the calcium-ite, the timing. I need a working description. Not a recipe for scholars. Something a Guild curing master could follow." She met the agent's eyes with the flat professional regard of someone who has calculated this conversation twelve times before having it. "I know what I am asking. I know what it would cost them. I am asking anyway, because the alternative costs me more."

## 11. Branch-Dependent Later Paragraphs

### Branch A -- Accept Path (Beat 2a: The Extraction)

Carin Harken walked the agent through the second curing shed as though showing a friend a garden. She pointed out where the calcium-ite deposit surfaced through the floor stones -- a pale vein of mineral that the family had chipped at for three generations, always careful to take only what they needed, always careful to pack the exposed face with wet clay afterward so the air would not degrade it. She explained the timing: the fish had to enter the first brine at the turn of the neap tide, not because of any mystical property of the water but because the mineral concentration in the tidal pools shifted with the moon, and the curing depended on a precise balance of salt and calcium-ite that only held for three days out of every fourteen.

Her daughter Lenne brought tea and dried plums. Carin talked while she worked, her hands moving through the process the way a musician's hands move through a piece they have played ten thousand times -- automatic, precise, beautiful in a way she was not conscious of. She told the agent about her grandmother, who had discovered the technique by accident when a storm surge flooded the wrong pool at the wrong time and produced fish that lasted through an entire winter without spoiling. She told the agent about the year the deposit almost ran out, and the three months of rationing that followed while the family searched for a new vein.

The agent listened. And the agent recorded -- not the way a guest remembers a host's generosity, but the way a surveyor records a claim. The details accumulated in neat columns in a journal that would leave Brinewall before the next tide.

Outside, the seabirds were loud and the sun made the tidal pools flash like mirrors, and the whole settlement smelled of salt and clean stone and the particular sharp sweetness of calcium-ite that Carin had known since she was old enough to be carried into the curing sheds on her mother's hip.

### Branch B -- Refuse Path (Beat 2b: The Refusal and Its Wake)

"No."

The word sat between them on the table like a stone dropped into still water. Tessaly did not move for a moment. Then she nodded, once, and closed the ledger. She did not close it with anger or emphasis. She closed it the way someone closes a book they will not need again.

"I understand," she said. Her voice was the same measured professional instrument it had been from the beginning, but something underneath it had gone quiet -- the way a fire goes quiet when the last good log burns through. She tucked the ledger into the oilskin case she carried against her ribs and buckled it shut.

"You should know that my asking you was the clean version. I have other approaches. They are slower and less reliable and they involve people who do not eat the Harken family's plums while they work." She was not threatening. She was informing, with the weary precision of someone who has already calculated the uglier paths and does not enjoy the arithmetic.

"If you hear that someone is asking questions in Brinewall about the curing sheds, it will not be because I sent them. But it will be because I was not the only person who noticed what the Harkens have." She stood and adjusted the case against her ribs. "The technique has perhaps five years before someone with more patience and less conscience than me puts it together. I was offering your people the chance to choose the terms. That offer is closed."

She left money on the table for the tea -- exact change, as though imprecision in small things was a luxury she could no longer afford -- and walked out through the caravanserai's east door into the sound of the drovers and the smith and the ordinary northern light.

## 12. Aftermath Paragraph

### Accept Path Aftermath

The map arrived by courier three days after the technique left Brinewall. Tessaly was true to her word -- the shrine's location was precise, annotated with route notes, guardian schedules, and a margin sketch of the approach from the river side that showed a path through the rocks that would be invisible to anyone who did not know to look for it. It was the work of seventeen years compressed into six pages of careful hand.

In Brinewall, nothing changed immediately. The tidal pools still flashed at neap tide. Carin Harken still walked the curing sheds with her daughter. The fish still came in silver and left in amber and fed the northern coast through the long winters. But in a warehouse in Greywater, a Guild curing master was already reading a description of the calcium-ite deposit and the timing of the tides, and his assistants were drawing up orders for experimental pools that would be built before the autumn storms. The first competing product would reach market within two seasons. Brinewall would notice when the prices dropped. They would investigate. They would find the trail.

The god had gained a weapon and spent a friendship. Whether the arithmetic balanced depended on the kind of god they were becoming.

### Refuse Path Aftermath

The shrine in the Vessen uplands continued to grow in its hidden valley, fed by prayers the player's agents could not hear and sustained by a rival whose patience had not yet been tested. The intelligence gap remained -- not as an absence the player could see, but as a shape in the dark that other gods could navigate and the player could not.

In Brinewall, the fish came in and the fish went out. Carin Harken walked the curing sheds with her daughter, and the calcium-ite held, and the monopoly held, and the settlement's small sovereignty continued for reasons no one in the settlement would ever fully understand. A god had refused to sell them and they would never know it.

That was the nature of the kindness: invisible, unmeasured, and impossible to spend. Whether it was also wise was a question only the rival god's next move could answer.

## 13. Aftermath Reaction Choices

### Accept Path Reactions

**Reaction A: "Prepare Brinewall for what is coming."**
The player directs their agent to quietly help Brinewall diversify before the price crash hits. New trade goods, new skills, new economic connections. This does not undo the betrayal, but it softens the landing. Brinewall may survive the loss of their monopoly if they have alternatives ready. This choice preserves the player's investment in Brinewall's survival while accepting the guilt of having caused the crisis.
*Future thread preserved: Brinewall's economic adaptation arc. The settlement becomes a test case for whether a god who harms an ally can also save them.*

**Reaction B: "Let the consequences land. Spend the intelligence."**
The player turns their full attention to the rival shrine. The shrine location is the prize; Brinewall's future is the cost already paid. This choice leans into the strategic posture that made the trade possible in the first place. The player invests in exploiting the advantage rather than mitigating the damage.
*Future thread preserved: The rival shrine confrontation arc. The intelligence is deployed immediately and aggressively.*

### Refuse Path Reactions

**Reaction A: "Find the shrine another way."**
The player directs agents to begin independent intelligence work on the rival shrine's location. Slower, harder, more expensive in agent time and attention -- but clean. This choice accepts the tactical disadvantage and commits resources to overcoming it.
*Future thread preserved: An intelligence-gathering arc that may produce future encounters with other information sources, including potentially circling back to Tessaly under different terms.*

**Reaction B: "Watch Tessaly. She will lead us to trouble."**
The player assigns an agent to monitor Tessaly's movements. If she pursues the technique through other channels, the player will know and can intervene to protect Brinewall directly. This choice turns the refusal into an active defensive posture.
*Future thread preserved: A counter-intelligence arc centered on Tessaly's desperation and the Greywater Guild's interest in Brinewall.*

### Branch Seduction Self-Check

**Branch A (Accept the trade):**
- *Why would a god choose this on purpose?* Because the rival shrine is a real strategic threat, and information advantages win wars that swords cannot. The settlement's monopoly has a finite lifespan anyway. A strategic god spends declining assets for appreciating ones.
- *What fantasy of interference does it offer?* The fantasy of divine realpolitik -- the god who makes hard trades in private and lives with the cost. The player gets to feel like a strategist who sees the whole board.
- *What value or future does it protect?* It protects the player's position in contested territory. It protects future agents from operating blind against a rival god. It protects the player's ability to act decisively.
- *If the labels were removed, would it still feel distinct?* Yes -- this branch is about sitting in someone's home and recording their family's secrets while they serve you tea. The sensory texture of the betrayal is vivid and specific.

**Branch B (Refuse the trade):**
- *Why would a god choose this on purpose?* Because trust is infrastructure. Allies who know their gods will not sell them are allies who take risks, share intelligence voluntarily, and remain loyal under pressure. A principled god builds durable networks.
- *What fantasy of interference does it offer?* The fantasy of divine integrity -- the god who could have taken the shortcut and chose the longer road. The player gets to feel like a deity whose word means something even when breaking it would be profitable.
- *What value or future does it protect?* It protects the player's relationship with Brinewall and, by extension, the principle that alliances are not expendable. It protects the player's reputation as a trustworthy patron in a world where trust is rare.
- *If the labels were removed, would it still feel distinct?* Yes -- this branch is about watching someone close a ledger and walk away into ordinary light, carrying her desperation with her, and knowing you could have helped her at someone else's expense and chose not to.

**Asymmetry check:**
- Neither branch is obviously more moral. The accept path is arguably pragmatic; the refuse path is arguably naive. Both have real costs and real rewards.
- The prose is balanced in quality between the branches -- both later paragraphs are written at the same standard.
- The aftermath rewards are different in kind, not in quantity -- intelligence versus trust, a weapon versus a friendship.
- Neither branch has a dramatically stronger scene. The extraction scene has intimacy and guilt; the refusal scene has quiet dignity and mounting dread.

### Primitive Gap Assessment

- **Remembered choice paths:** The encounter requires the system to remember which branch the player chose at beat 1 and deliver different beat 2 prose, different aftermath, and different follow-on hooks. This is the basic branching capability that the encounter system must support. If the runtime cannot resolve branch-dependent prose at step 2, this is a primitive gap. Documented per checklist.
- **Betrayal mark / ticking social consequence:** The accept path creates a hidden mark that becomes visible when a future encounter triggers investigation. This requires a persistent hidden-state attachment that can be queried by future encounters. If this primitive does not exist, the betrayal's delayed consequences cannot fire. This is a potential `blocked-primitive` for the delayed-revelation mechanic, though the mark itself can be implemented as a standard attachment with a visibility flag.
- **Intelligence attachment with structured data:** The shrine location reward is not a simple reputation bonus -- it is a structured intelligence object with map data, routes, and guardian schedules that future encounters and agent decisions should be able to query. If intelligence attachments are limited to simple flags, the richness of this reward is degraded. This is an `author-now` item that should be designed as a real persistent world object.

All gaps are manageable within the existing system or require `author-now` work, not fundamental runtime changes.

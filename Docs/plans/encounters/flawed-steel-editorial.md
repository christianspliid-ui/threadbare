> Scale: medium | Slug: flawed-steel | Pass: editorial
> Date: 2026-04-04 | Pipeline version: 1.0

## 1. Prose Quality

### Opening Paragraph (Section 10)

The opening is strong. It earns its length. The cold forge as a missing heartbeat, the demand letter on the anvil where hot steel should be, the breathing through the coal shed wall -- these are specific, sensory, and emotionally loaded images that do real work establishing the scene's emotional temperature before a single plot point is stated.

Two passages deserve attention:

**Weak passage 1:** "written in the careful hand of someone who had been taught that contracts are more binding than swords."

This is a competent character-sketch sentence, but it explains too much. It tells the reader what kind of person Torve is through an authorial caption rather than letting the letter itself do the work. The line would land harder if it stayed in the physical.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE] "written in a hand steady enough that the ink had not bled once, even on the words that were threats."

**Weak passage 2:** "the particular stillness of someone who did not trust what they might do if they moved."

This is good -- it shows restraint as a physical act. No revision needed, but flagging it as a sentence that sets the prose register for the encounter. The rest of the opening should match this quality. The second paragraph ("Maren stood with her back to the shed door") does. The third paragraph -- the god's perception of threads -- is slightly more abstract and slightly less physical. It works as transition into the choice space but it drifts toward summarizing what the reader has already felt.

### Branch-Dependent Paragraphs (Section 11)

**Branch A** is the stronger passage. The militia captain stepping aside when he sees Maren's face; Torve sharpening a knife that does not need sharpening; the forge-master's voice that "carried without being raised" -- these are physical, character-driven details that trust the reader.

One weak line in Branch A: "one old enough to have earned every scar on her hands, one young enough that the scars were still forming." The parallelism is neat, but it is a writerly observation imposed on the scene rather than something Torve would actually perceive. Torve would see a forge-master and a girl. The symmetry is the author's voice intruding.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE] "She looked up as the gate opened and saw the forge-master walking toward her with a young woman trailing half a step behind, blinking against the light like someone pulled out of a cellar."

**Branch B** is good but slightly overwritten in one area. The opening metaphor -- "a whisper threaded through the morning air, settling into the gaps between what Maren Ironhewn knew and what she was willing to say aloud" -- is precise and does work. However, the parenthetical analogy mid-paragraph is too self-conscious:

**Weak passage:** "Some truths were load-bearing; remove them and the whole structure collapses."

This reads like the author stepping in with a thesis statement. The forge metaphor is available and more natural to the scene.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE] "Some truths held the rest of the structure up the way a tang holds a blade. Pull one and the whole thing comes apart in your hands."

The rest of Branch B is strong. The final image -- "the tremor in Maren's hands when she closed the ledger" -- is excellent. The line "The numbers in it were true. They were also incomplete." is clean, sharp, and earns its brevity.

### Aftermath Paragraphs (Section 12)

**Branch A aftermath** is solid but runs slightly long. The sentence about Dalla -- "walked out through the eastern gate under Torve's supervision to begin the season of service that would clear her debt, or at least transform it into something that could be measured in labor rather than shame" -- is doing too much in one clause. The "or at least transform it" qualification weakens the landing.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE] "walked out through the eastern gate under Torve's supervision, to begin the season of labor that would become the shape of her debt."

**Branch B aftermath** is the best prose in the entire draft. The line about the letter of reference -- "It was the kindest thing she had done for the girl, and it was also a lie, and she knew that both of those things were true simultaneously" -- is genuinely moving and captures the moral texture of Branch B perfectly. The final image of the ember banked in ash is earned and scene-appropriate. The closing -- Maren's inspection thoroughness that "looked like mastery but felt, to her, like penance" -- is the encounter's best sentence. It would be the passage a player remembers.

**Overall prose assessment:** Above the quality bar. The draft has a distinct narrative voice -- restrained, physical, trusting of concrete detail. A few moments of authorial over-explanation dilute it, but these are fixable in revision. The Branch B aftermath is exceptional and sets the tone that the rest of the encounter should aspire to.

## 2. Branch Seduction Audit

### Branch A: Forge the Truth

**Moral asymmetry:** Present. This is the path of a god who believes pain endured openly heals faster than pain hidden. It asks the player to watch a mortal they could protect from humiliation walk into it voluntarily -- and to feel that this is the right thing.

**Dramatic asymmetry:** Present. Branch A is louder, more public, more physically present. Dalla is in the scene. Torve has to face the actual person who defrauded her. The militia captain is watching. There are witnesses. This branch has more characters on stage and more social risk.

**Information asymmetry:** Present. In Branch A, all parties have the full picture. There is nothing hidden. The dramatic tension comes from how the parties respond to the truth, not from managing who knows what.

**Prose asymmetry:** Present. Branch A's prose is rawer, more confrontational, more physical. The gate opening. The walk across camp. Torve setting down a knife. The register is honest-brutal.

**Aftermath asymmetry:** Present. The aftermath is institutional -- guild reform, public reckoning, Dalla's fate decided in the open. The settlement remembers a wound that scarred cleanly. The reaction choices are about stewardship: does the god trust the institution to reform itself, or does the god stay close to the mortal?

**Verdict on Branch A seduction:** Strong. A player who values transparency, accountability, and the hard dignity of facing consequences will choose this path without feeling manipulated. The branch earns its appeal through narrative texture, not through being secretly optimal.

### Branch B: Temper the Narrative

**Moral asymmetry:** Present. This is the path of a god who understands that institutions are maintained by controlled information, and that sometimes the kinder thing is to let the story be simpler than the truth. It asks the player to become complicit in a deception -- and to feel that this, too, is a form of care.

**Dramatic asymmetry:** Present. Branch B is quieter, more intimate, more psychologically loaded. Dalla is absent. The tension is between what is said and what is unsaid. The god is the invisible third actor in a scene between two professionals. The register is measured, controlled -- and slightly unnerving because the reader knows the control is manufactured.

**Information asymmetry:** Present and central. Branch B is about who knows what. The player knows everything. Torve does not. Maren knows but cannot say. The dramatic tension comes entirely from the gap between truth and presentation.

**Prose asymmetry:** Present. Branch B's prose is more careful, more syntactically controlled, with longer sentences that mirror the deliberate presentation Maren is making. The tremor in her hands is the crack in the surface. The register is professional-evasive.

**Aftermath asymmetry:** Present. The aftermath is personal and latent -- a hidden mark, a disappeared apprentice, a lie that is also a kindness. The settlement does not remember a crisis. The reaction choices are about what to do with concealed truth: let it lie, or prepare for the day it surfaces. These are different philosophical stances from Branch A's choices, and they feel genuinely different.

**Verdict on Branch B seduction:** Strong. A player who values pragmatic mercy, stability, and the complex recognition that not all truths serve the people they hurt will choose this path. Branch B is not the "dark" path or the "easy" path -- it is the path where the god gets its hands dirty out of something that genuinely looks like wisdom.

**Cross-branch assessment:** Neither branch is obviously correct, obviously stronger, or obviously the "real" choice. Both have genuine appeal, genuine cost, and genuine long-term consequence. The seduction audit passes.

## 3. Branch Count Assessment

Two branches is correct for this encounter.

The draft already addresses this: the Reveal / Contain / Displace template would naturally suggest a third branch (Displace -- move the problem elsewhere), but the draft deliberately excludes it because it would be weaker at medium scale. This is good editorial judgment. A third branch that reads as "dump the problem on another settlement" or "make Dalla someone else's concern" would dilute the encounter's tight focus on the forge-master's personal reckoning and the god's relationship to honesty.

The branching templates document explicitly states that two branches is "often ideal for medium encounters" and that a third branch "should be earned, not assumed." This encounter earns two strong branches. A third would be symmetry-driven, not dramatically justified.

No branches should be added. No branches should be cut.

## 4. Scale Discipline Check

**Medium scale is correctly applied.** The encounter has:
- A clear local scope (one settlement, one forge, one mercenary company)
- A 3-beat structure (discovery, intervention, aftermath)
- Two branches with full prose for each
- An aftermath with two reaction choices per branch
- Named NPCs with individual fates
- Future hooks that are local (guild reform, hidden truth) rather than regional or faction-wide

The encounter does not over-reach into long-encounter territory. There is no second intervention point, no mid-encounter twist, no faction-level political consequence. The stakes are real -- people may die carrying bad steel -- but the resolution is community-scale. The reward weight (reputation channels, one potential mercenary ally, one forge-master relationship) is medium-weight.

The only area where the draft leans slightly large is the outcome ladder, which has five tiers per branch. This is thorough rather than bloated -- the tiers are well-differentiated and each tells a distinct micro-story -- but during implementation, the author should ensure the ladder does not expand the encounter's runtime beyond what "medium" should feel like at the table.

Scale discipline: PASS.

## 5. Inspiration Anchor Honesty

The draft's "What the Inspiration Actually Changed" section (end of Section 1) makes three specific claims. Each deserves independent verification:

**Claim 1: Without the Betrayal Revealed archetype, this would have been a simple negotiation.**

Credible. The mercenary grievance alone is a two-party negotiation. The archetype pushed Dalla into the center, making the encounter a three-party problem where the betrayal is the wound and the negotiation is the pressure. The apprentice's presence is what creates the choice architecture -- without her, there is no honest/managed truth split. The archetype genuinely reshaped the encounter from a commerce dispute into a moral reckoning.

**Claim 2: Without the Builder's Dilemma, the encounter would only focus on the present crisis.**

Credible. The deployed-weapons thread -- blades already in the world that cannot be recalled -- is the encounter's emotional depth charge. It transforms the crisis from "fix this mess" to "live with the knowledge that your work is already killing people you will never meet." This thread appears in the opening paragraph ("how many other blades bearing Maren's stamp had already gone into the world") and haunts the aftermath. Without it, the encounter would be a local scandal. With it, the encounter has a layer of irreversible consequence that justifies its emotional weight.

**Claim 3: Without the Dilemma Library's axiological framing, the branches would have been "help" vs. "don't help."**

Credible. The Mercy vs. Ruthlessness and Honesty vs. Cunning axes are visible in the branch architecture. Branch A is not "help more" and Branch B is not "help less" -- they are genuinely different philosophies of divine intervention. The axiological framing gave the branches a moral grammar, not just a tactical one.

**Anchor honesty: PASS.** The cited anchors demonstrably changed the encounter's structure, emotional texture, and choice architecture. These are not decorative citations.

## 6. Aftermath Payoff

**Branch A aftermath** lands with the right emotional weight. The image of Maren on the step with a cup she is not drinking; Dalla walking through the gate without looking back; the guild beginning its audit. The payoff is bittersweet-honest: the crisis is resolved, but at a cost the settlement will feel for a season. The reaction choices -- "Let the guild carry it forward" vs. "Stay close to Maren" -- are clear and meaningfully different. One is institutional trust, the other is personal patronage.

**Branch B aftermath** lands harder. The letter of reference that is both the kindest thing and a lie. The forge relighting. The ember banked in ash. The penance disguised as mastery. This is the encounter's emotional high point, and it lands because the prose earns it. The reaction choices -- "Let the silence hold" vs. "Prepare for the reckoning" -- are genuinely different stances toward concealed truth. One accepts the moral residue permanently; the other acknowledges that what was buried will eventually surface and prepares for it.

**One concern:** The Branch A reaction choices are slightly less evocative than the Branch B reactions. "Let the guild carry it forward" is clear but dry. "Stay close to Maren" is warmer but could be more specific about what that closeness means in practice.

[EDITORIAL REWRITE for Branch A, first reaction label] "Let the guild learn to govern itself." -- More active, more clearly a philosophical stance (trust mortals to build their own oversight).

[EDITORIAL REWRITE for Branch A, second reaction label] "Keep the forge-master close." -- "Close" is more intimate and more honest about what the god is doing (claiming a mortal, not just helping one).

**Aftermath payoff: PASS with minor revisions.** Both aftermath paths land emotionally and narratively. The reaction choices are clear and philosophically distinct. The Branch A labels could be slightly sharpened to match the quality of the Branch B labels.

## 7. Dilemma Energy

The dilemma is real. This is not a binary between "good option" and "bad option with a dark-side bonus." Both branches protect something the player cares about and sacrifice something the player cares about.

**Branch A sacrifices:** Maren's dignity (public humiliation), Dalla's agency (her fate decided in the open by people with power over her), the settlement's short-term stability (institutional audit, disrupted craft output, visible crisis).

**Branch A protects:** Institutional integrity, community trust in the long term, Torve's respect (which may become a genuine alliance), the settlement's ability to face future crises honestly.

**Branch B sacrifices:** Honesty (the god becomes complicit in managed truth), Torve's right to know what happened to her soldiers (a genuine moral cost), the forge-master's inner peace (she will carry this), and long-term stability (the hidden mark is a time bomb).

**Branch B protects:** Maren's public reputation, Dalla's chance at a clean departure, the settlement's confidence in its own defenses, the forge's economic function, the short-term absence of institutional disruption.

The sacrifices are real and the protections are real. Neither branch is cost-free. Neither branch is secretly optimal. A player who chooses Branch A is a different kind of god than a player who chooses Branch B, and both kinds of god have a defensible position.

**Dilemma energy: HIGH.** This is a genuine choice between two philosophies of divine interference, not a difficulty slider.

## 8. Experience Differentiator Gate

### Scene & Prose

**1. Does the opening paragraph place the player inside a moment already in motion?**
YES. The forge is cold. The demand has been delivered. Dalla is locked in the shed. The militia is at the walls. The player arrives into the middle of a crisis, not the beginning of one.

**2. Does the prose have its own voice -- cadence, rhythm, sentence variety?**
YES. Short declaratives ("She had not turned around since locking it.") alternate with longer descriptive passages. The third paragraph uses a tripartite rhythm ("Maren's pride, fraying. Dalla's shame, hardening into something that might become defiance if left long enough. Torve's patience, measured in hours.") that is distinctive and controlled. There are a few moments where the voice slips into over-explanation (see Prose Quality section), but the baseline voice is strong.

**3. Does the scene prose name the specific elements that later become choices?**
YES. Maren's pride, Dalla's shame, Torve's patience, and the question of blades already in the world are all named in the opening. The choice between honesty and managed truth is seeded by the forge-master's stillness -- she has not decided yet, and the god's intervention is precisely about which way she tips.

**4. Would a reader feel something from the prose alone?**
YES. The cold forge as a missing heartbeat. The breathing through the wall. The hands that hang still because their owner does not trust what they might do. The fifty soldiers camped outside the gate for the simple, terrible reason that their weapons will break. This is prose that carries emotional weight independent of any game mechanic.

### Choices & Intervention

**5. Does each approach card have its own prose paragraph?**
YES. Section 11 provides full Beat 2 opening paragraphs for both branches. Branch A's paragraph is 167 words of scene-setting. Branch B's paragraph is 226 words. Both are written as fiction, not as mechanical descriptions.

**6. Does each approach card narratively justify its cost?**
YES. Branch A's cost is visible in the prose: Maren's public humiliation, the militia captain stepping aside, Dalla blinking in the light. Branch B's cost is visible in the tremor of Maren's hands and the incomplete ledger. Neither cost is stated as a line item; both are dramatized.

**7. Does each approach card include a narrative risk?**
YES. Branch A's risk: the negotiation may collapse because the full truth is too much (the outcome ladder's failure states reflect this). Branch B's risk: the concealment may unravel, and if it does, the fallout is worse than honest disclosure (the hidden mark mechanic and the outcome ladder's failure states reflect this).

**8. Are the choice labels scene-specific rather than generic god-verbs?**
YES. "Forge the Truth" and "Temper the Narrative" are specific to the setting (a forge), use metallurgical metaphor (forging, tempering), and describe what the god is actually doing in the scene. They are not "Inspire" or "Manipulate" or "Command."

**9. Do the choices feel like graduated options, not a binary toggle?**
YES. The two branches are not "help" and "don't help," or "good" and "evil." They are two philosophies of intervention -- honest reckoning vs. managed resolution -- each with its own moral grammar, cost structure, and long-term consequence. The graduation is in philosophy, not in magnitude.

### Aftermath & Consequence

**10. Does the aftermath have its own prose?**
YES. Section 12 provides full aftermath paragraphs for both branches. Branch A's aftermath is 149 words. Branch B's aftermath is 192 words. Both are reflective, scene-grounded, and written as fiction.

**11. Are consequence outcomes actor-centered with names/faces?**
YES. Every consequence is attached to a named character: Maren's reputation, Dalla's fate, Torve's disposition, the guild elders' response. There are no anonymous stat deltas.

**12. Does the aftermath offer reaction choices for the player?**
YES. Two reaction choices per branch (Section 13), each with a clear explanation of what future thread is preserved and what is released.

**13. Do aftermath reaction choices represent different philosophical stances?**
YES. Branch A: institutional self-governance ("the guild carries it forward") vs. divine patronage ("stay close to Maren"). Branch B: acceptance of concealment ("let the silence hold") vs. strategic preparation ("prepare for the reckoning"). Each pair represents a genuinely different posture toward the aftermath.

### Presentation

**14. Does the Presentation Kit specify concept art?**
YES. Section 16 describes scene composition, palette (cold iron grays, amber of banked coals), mood (stillness before a reckoning), and Threadbare aesthetic direction. The description is specific enough for art direction.

**Result: 14/14 YES.** No automatic REVISE triggers.

## 10. Revision Summary

### Must Fix

None. There are no structural, branching, or experience-differentiator failures.

### Should Fix

1. **Opening paragraph, Torve's letter description:** Replace the character-caption line ("written in the careful hand of someone who had been taught that contracts are more binding than swords") with something that stays in the physical register. See editorial rewrite in Section 1.

2. **Branch A, Beat 2 paragraph:** Replace the over-neat age-and-scars parallelism ("one old enough to have earned every scar on her hands, one young enough that the scars were still forming") with a less symmetrical, more Torve-grounded observation. See editorial rewrite in Section 1.

3. **Branch B, Beat 2 paragraph:** Replace the thesis-statement metaphor ("Some truths were load-bearing; remove them and the whole structure collapses") with a forge-native equivalent. See editorial rewrite in Section 1.

4. **Branch A aftermath:** Tighten Dalla's departure sentence -- the "or at least transform it" qualification weakens the landing. See editorial rewrite in Section 1.

5. **Branch A reaction choice labels:** Sharpen from "Let the guild carry it forward" / "Stay close to Maren" to "Let the guild learn to govern itself" / "Keep the forge-master close" (or equivalents that are more active and more clearly philosophical stances). See editorial rewrite in Section 6.

### Consider

1. **Opening paragraph, third paragraph:** The god's-eye thread enumeration ("Maren's pride, fraying. Dalla's shame, hardening...") is effective but slightly more abstract than the first two paragraphs. Consider whether one more physical detail -- a sound, a smell, a texture -- could ground it further before transitioning to the choice space.

2. **Branch B aftermath, ember metaphor:** The ember banked in ash is earned and good. But it is also the kind of metaphor that can become a crutch if the same encounter family reuses it in the follow-on "truth surfaces" encounter. Flag it as a one-time image.

3. **Outcome ladder depth:** Five tiers per branch is thorough and well-differentiated, but monitor during implementation to ensure the encounter's runtime stays within medium-scale expectations. If critical failure feels like it belongs to a longer encounter, consider simplifying it.

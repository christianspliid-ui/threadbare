Encounters & Aftermath

An encounter is not a die roll dressed in prose. It is a framed chapter — the game pulls the camera onto one threaded mortal's crisis and asks the only question that matters: what kind of god are you, toward this?

An encounter is a framed chapterThe curated moment at the heart of the three-beat turn — one mortal's situation, crystallised, with you looking down on it.

Most of what your mortals do happens quietly, in the churn of the simulation. An encounter is when that churn cresting into a moment — a threaded mortal's situation sharpens to a point, and the game frames it for you as a chapter. You read the prose. You weigh what is at stake. And at the critical step you play whichever nudge cards the encounter authored for exactly this situation — or you play none and let fate decide, which is always a real choice, and on a step nobody authored a hand for it is the only one.

What you are never doing is buying odds (THR-1121, 2026-08-15). Until then, any step without an authored hand fell back to a generic three-stance menu — support for one essence, coerce for five, withdraw for nothing — each quoting a flat percentage it added to the roll. That was the pre-nudge divine-intervention layer, and it is retired: the generic menu is gone, the percentage is gone, and no choice anywhere adds to the roll on account of what it cost. A god nudges the fiction; fate picks the outcome. A step that authored no hand now says so plainly and offers Let fate decide alone — the model working, not a gap.

One encounter kept the old stance labels a little longer, and it is worth saying why (THR-1123, 2026-08-15). Gate Duty — the checkpoint at a town's gate — never authored its own moves. It decorated the generic three: every stance label, intent paragraph, aftermath recap and consequence line was written per step and then hung on support, coerce or withdraw. Retiring the generic menu would therefore have deleted a fully-written encounter's entire move set as a side effect, so the three stances were kept alive locally for one release while the real conversion was done. Gate Duty now deals its own cards, three to a step and different at each: you steady the courier, force the captain, or keep your hand folded while the queue draws taut; later you measure the seizure, break the courier open, or give the witness the scene. The fiction is the same fiction — it was always this encounter's, never the menu's. What changed is that it is now attached to cards the encounter owns, and the aftermath keys on which card you played rather than on which of three generic postures it resembled.

Encounters are the middle beat of the turn. The order around them is deliberate:

Scan — you choose whose story to watch → The encounter — the chapter → Aftermath — the breath before the next scan

The encounter is the chapter, not the roll. Encounters are the payoff of the threads you weave. A quiet early world may go many turns between chapters; a god with a dozen threads in motion — or a world deep into its doom — will find them cresting far more often. The game never rations your chapters; it helps you hold them. Every encounter, running or finished, stays readable in the Chapter Ledger. (Turn order and rhythm are the The Three-Beat Turn page; here we care about the moment itself.)

Two shapes, one formatEncounters come branching or recurring. They share a single underlying format and split only on how they are made.

Every encounter — no matter its shape — is built from the same underlying definition. What differs is authorship and how it enters the world:

Branching encounters

Hand-authored, multi-step chapters with real forks — your choice sends the mortal down a genuinely different path, not the same path in a different colour. These are the set-piece stories, written one at a time with full aftermath.

Recurring (template) encounters

Systemic moments — guild, social, tavern, combat, borderland — that fire again and again across the world, instantiated from templates that share a spine but vary in who, where, and why. The texture of a living world in motion.

You will rarely need to tell them apart in play. They read the same, they resolve the same, and they close the same way. The distinction matters to the people who write them, not to the god who lives them.

You see the hex, you see everything on itAwareness is hex-granular — the unit of "what can I perceive" is the whole tile, not the individual encounter.

An encounter is only yours to act on if you are aware of it — and awareness works at the level of the hex. If you can see a tile, you see everything on it: every location, every sublocation, every encounter cresting anywhere across it. There is no fog within a hex you can already see.

Whether you can see a distant hex is a matter of reach: the game measures the hex-coordinate distance from where your attention sits to the tile in question, and compares it against how far your awareness carries in that domain. Close enough, and the chapter surfaces to you. Too far, and it unfolds unwatched — the world does not wait for a god who was looking elsewhere.

Awareness never hides content from you as a design lever

Awareness is about where your attention reaches on the map, not about the game withholding options to create mystery. Once a hex is in view, its encounters are fully legible — the game never uses "you don't know enough yet" to hide a choice. Knowing more can enrich a moment, but it never gates one away.

One curve for peasants and godsEvery attempt in the game resolves the same way — a capability score bent through a curve into a probability, then a single roll.

There is exactly one resolution engine in Threadbearer, and it governs everyone. A farmhand trying to talk down a mob and an Ascendant trying to bind an entropy well run through the same machine: the relevant capability is bent through a smooth curve into a probability of success, and then a single hidden roll settles it. No special dice for gods. No separate combat system. One spine, everywhere.

Your own castings run on it too. They used to be exempt — a player card simply succeeded, and the price the content author had written into it was thrown away. Now a casting that carries difficulty rolls like anything else, against your Domain Capability in that Reach. Two asymmetries remain, and both are deliberate: a casting authored at zero difficulty (the divine.* soul-verbs, your reach signatures, observation) still always lands; and a paid casting can never outright fail — a losing roll is caught at success-at-cost, so the working arrives crooked rather than not at all. The upside is untouched, so a clean success or a surge still has to be earned. One curve, one ladder, one floor the god alone stands on.

What you feel of this is deliberately soft. The game does not show you a percentage or a die. It shows you a situation and lets the prose carry the odds — a mortal who is clearly outmatched reads as outmatched. The roll resolves into one of a few outcome bands, and the band decides how the chapter turns:

Critical success

Beyond what you hoped — a moment that echoes forward.

Success

The intervention lands. The path bends your way.

Failure

It doesn't take — but the story moves anyway.

Critical failure

It rebounds — a scar, a grudge, a new tension.

Between a clean win and a clean miss there is also room for success at a cost — the intervention lands, but it takes something with it. And the critical swings are a matter of the dice doubling on themselves: when the hidden roll comes up matched (11, 22, and so on) the outcome tips to its extreme — a triumph that echoes, or a failure that wounds.

Because the same curve governs the mighty and the meek, an underdog's win is never impossible and a favourite's loss is never off the table. That is the point: a world where the odds always held would have no chapters worth reading.

The nudge — how you touch a sceneYou bend the physics of the moment. Fate still picks what comes out of it.

When a chapter has your full attention, you are not reduced to watching. The step in front of you carries a small hand of nudge cards — written for that scene, not drawn from a global deck — and each one names something concrete you can do to tilt the moment. Each costs essence. Each shifts the odds a stated amount. And then the dice are still thrown.

This is the whole shape of your power stated in one mechanic: you do not choose what the mortal does, and you never choose how it comes out. You change what is likely. A card that promises an outcome would make you the author of the scene rather than a pressure on it.

Only where you are looking

Nudges exist only in the attended encounter — the chapter you have pulled the camera onto. Encounters running in the background or merely being steered resolve exactly as they always have: no pause, no hand, no modifiers. The nudge is a feature of your attention, not of the world.

A card may also carry a rider — a promise about the bands, never about the roll. There are three. One holds back a critical failure, turning a catastrophe into an ordinary miss. One floors a failure (or a near miss) at success-at-a-price. The third — the Gambit — widens both ends: a good result gets better and a bad one gets worse. At most one rider applies to a step: the strongest, never a stack, and the two protective riders both outrank the Gambit, so a hand can never accidentally cancel its own safety net. Riders remap the band after the roll has already landed — they never take an extra die, so the same world always rolls the same way whether or not you paid.

Beyond the odds, a card can carry a price in another currency and a grant. The Heavy Hand helps openly and rivals notice; the Veil gives the same help unwitnessed; the Bargain costs nothing in essence and pushes the doom clock instead. And a card may leave something behind — an omen staining the region, a condition lifted, a blade left in the reeds, an ambition kindled in someone who had none. What a card grants is not a special card language: it is the same vocabulary an encounter's own aftermath speaks, dispatched by the same machinery, so a card reaches the system that owns the change rather than a second path to the same place.

That first rider is worth naming plainly, because it is the single exception to a rule the rest of the game holds without flinching: critical failure survives at every scale — except on a step where a god has paid to hold it back. It is per-step, priced, and authored into the scene. It is not a difficulty setting, and it is not something a mortal can ever do for themselves.

Some cards are gated: on a sphere you can draw on, on a god-power you have earned, or on a trait the mortal happens to hold. A card you merely cannot afford is shown to you greyed, with its price — that is a goal. A card the mortal could never qualify for is not shown at all — that would only be noise.

Aftermath reshapes the mortal, not youResolution lands, and the consequences are written into the mortal's life — their traits, their bonds, their reputation, the book of their run.

When the roll settles, the encounter does not simply end — it closes. The aftermath is the last beat: the resolution lands, the world graph shifts, and a reaction is chosen — by you, or, if you let the chapter run unattended, by the encounter itself falling into its default. That reaction writes real consequences: it can shift the mortal's traits, thicken or fray a thread, move reputation, and leave a chronicle entry the mortal carries for the rest of the run.

The crucial thing to hold onto: aftermath reshapes the protagonist's trajectory, not the god's. You do not level up. The mortal you touched walks away changed — braver or broken, indebted or embittered — and that change is what the next chapter is built on.

The closing beat is written per band, so the same quest does not sign off the same way twice. A vigil kept clean and a vigil kept at the price of a wound reach different closing paragraphs, name different consequences, and offer you different things to do about it — and the ones you will see least are the ones written most carefully. A triumph that echoes or a failure that wounds is, by the nature of the dice, a rare reading; an encounter that only ever wrote its middle would show you its blandest ending most often and its best ones never.

What the ending changed is named in four words, and they are the same four in every encounter in the game — so the vocabulary is learned once and read forever. A Scar is what the trial cost them, on body or spirit. A Bond is who now stands with them, or against them. A Boon is what they earned, and why. A Path is a way that has opened and that the game now tracks — a road they know, an offer that will come round again, a meeting already on its way. Each consequence names the thing that changed, shows roughly how far it moved, and, where an author wrote one, says what in the scene caused it: "Caught at the rail by a passing wanderer — Jorun the Wayfarer walks with her now." A consequence that names no cause you watched happen is a content bug. Where the ending granted something real — a wound, a blessing, a curse, a borrowed power — the thing it names is clickable, and opens that attachment's own page (THR-1120). What you reach is the attachment as it is written, not the copy on the person: the grant is made when you pick a reaction, and the ending closes on that pick, so at the moment you are reading the chip nothing has been written to anyone yet. The held copy, with its remaining term, appears on the bearer's own sheet afterwards. A consequence that granted nothing you could hold — standing shifting, a reach growing — stays plain text rather than offering a link into an empty page.

Every consequence names state the game actually wrote (Law 56, THR-1141, 2026-08-16). This is the rule the four words rest on, and it is why Path above reads differently than it used to. A consequence is not a place to put a nice sentence: it is a receipt. If the ending tells you a ford upstream is now a real option, the mortal is carrying a knowledge record about that ford, and a later scene can read it. If it tells you the swindler will surface again, an encounter is already scheduled. If it tells you thirty people can name the stranger who walked them in, there is an edge to the caravan master and a standing on that road. The sweep that established this found 27 consequences across 11 encounters naming a change nothing in the simulation had ever heard of — among them the bridge reporting Path · The River Crossing on an ending whose only reaction did nothing at all.

The fix was never to delete the sentences. All 27 kept their prose and gained the write the prose claimed, because a consequence claiming something the world does not hold is usually the mark of an unfinished encounter rather than an overwritten one — the author saw the systemic connection and stopped one step short of wiring it. Where a sentence genuinely earns no connection it belongs in the closing paragraph, which promises nothing and is the right home for a mood. A machine gate now fails any consequence whose ending performs no qualifying write, so the shape cannot ship again in silence.

And the thing it names has to be reachable (Law 56 clause 2, THR-1153/THR-1164, 2026-08-17). A write behind the sentence was only half the promise. The bridge was repaired once under the rule above — a real knowledge record was added behind Path · The River Crossing — and the director played it and reported it still broken, because the words "the river crossing" and "the ford upstream" named landscape that is not a thing in the world. So the bar moved from backed to anchored: the noun on a consequence must name an object the game actually holds, and the player must be able to get to it. A sweep against that predicate found 48 more chips across 17 encounters naming a referent that carried no anchor at all.

Most anchors are written down plainly — an attachment names its own template, and that template is the same in every world. The ones that cannot be are written as a declaration instead: a short form naming how to find the thing, resolved against the live world at the moment the ending is drawn. $actor is the mortal the scene resolved for. $cast:<key> is a member of the scene's own cast. $faction:<defId> is an order — needed because a faction is minted fresh in each world and chapters of one order share a definition, so there is no fixed number an author could write for The Dawn, which nineteen shipped consequences name. An anchor that resolves to nothing is dropped rather than shown, because a link that goes nowhere is worse than a word that was never a link: it looks like it works until you click it.

Two judgements come with the rule, and both cut toward keeping content rather than deleting it. An object the encounter itself creates counts as existing — a spawned keepsake, a granted condition — so a consequence may name a thing that did not exist a moment ago. And a consequence about something with no page of its own anchors through its carrier: a scheduled return, a piece of knowledge, or a debt is reached through the person who carries it, and the sentence has to name that person. Where nothing real can be named at all, the sentence goes back to the closing paragraph and the chip goes away — the words survive, the false receipt does not.

Magnitude is shown, never printed. A short run of marks says how far a thing moved — one for slight, three for a great deal, and a single mark when the change has no scale at all, because a blade is carried or it is not. The precise wording sits under the mark for anyone who wants it; the glance-level answer is the shape.

Failure is plot, not punishment

A roll going against you is not a dead end — it is a hook. When an intervention fails, the aftermath's job is to make the next chapter more interesting: a scar that becomes a vendetta, a humiliation that hardens into resolve, a thread pulled taut instead of snapped. A flat "you failed, nothing happened" is a content bug, never a balance one. The world is supposed to be more interesting because you tried and missed.

The ending tells a story, not a ledger

A mortal's hands grow surer every time they are used, and their standing drifts with every scene they are seen in. None of that reaches the closing beat as a sentence. Those changes are real, they happen, and they are readable on the character's own sheet — but on the ending they are demoted to a mark and a glyph, because an encounter that spent a line on "her Stone grew steadily" spent it on the least interesting thing that happened. The consequences that get words are the ones the encounter was written to have: reserved, authored, and chosen because they were worth saying.

The shape of a good choiceThe best encounters do not ask you to succeed — they ask you to decide who you are willing to be.

The encounters that land hardest pose a dilemma in the plain sense: a moment where there is no clean win, only a choice between things you value. Steady the frightened heir and you make a ruler who leans on you; let them face the fear alone and you make one who never will. Both are "successes." Neither is free.

Underneath every well-made encounter are four rules its authors hold to. You never see them named, but you feel their effects — they are why a Threadbearer choice feels like a choice about character rather than a lever on a machine:

Path over adjective

Every choice changes the path the story takes — not just the colour of the same outcome.

The moral axis is structural

Each encounter's Reach carries a virtue↔vice axis (Iron: Protector ↔ Conqueror). Your choice tilts the mortal toward one pole.

Verbs are soft-power

You "stir her resolve," you don't "make her fight." A god nudges; it does not puppet. Every verb is a whisper, not a hand.

Every primitive is clickable

Every thing an encounter names — a place, an item, a faction, a rival — is itself a detail you can open and act on.

You are a god, never the character

Every choice in an encounter is a divine choice — to whisper, to steady, to withdraw, to strengthen. You never step into the mortal's shoes and act as them. "Let them handle it" is always on the table, and sometimes it is the truest thing a god can do.

One format: UnifiedActionTemplate IMPL

  • Every encounter — branching or linear — is a UnifiedActionTemplate (UAT), the single encounter format since THR-108. The older EncounterTemplate format is removed from the codebase; do not author or reference it. Templates are stored as action_template nodes and instantiated at a location for a specific agent.
  • The two shapes split on pipeline, not format: branching encounters live in src/data/encounters/ and use authored ActionStepBranch arms (exemplars rival-shrine-betrayal.ts, flawed-steel.ts); linear template families (guild/social/tavern/combat/borderland) are compiled via the template-encounter-rewrite skill and live in the src/data/*-encounter-content.ts files. ActionStepBranch is exclusive to branching encounters (THR-191).
  • A branch authored today could only ever take fallback — the mortal now decides it (THR-894, 2026-07-31). ActionStepBranch picks its variant by a recorded choiceId, and the only thing that ever recorded one was the retired player-pick (authoredChoices). So the fork was in the schema and unreachable in the world: every new branching encounter resolved down its fallback arm forever. The shipped pattern for a personality-driven fork lived in exactly one place — Meet The First (THR-868), where the mortal's value axes plus the net pole lean of the committed cards pick the direction a success writes while fate rolls how cleanly. That selector is now general. A branch may declare decidedBy: { axis } naming a ValuePair, and its variants key exactly 'positive' / 'negative' (the two poles, validated at build time — a typo'd key fails rather than silently dying in fallback, the THR-844 lesson). At the deciding step the engine reads the mortal's live axis position (their standing profile plus accumulated drift), adds the net poleLean of the cards the god committed on that step, and records the resulting pole through the existing choice-history path — so no second branch-resolution route exists to drift out of sync. The player never picks. They lean; the mortal chooses; and the choice sticks — the decided pole drifts the mortal toward the pole they took, which is what makes repeated choices become character. A hand with no leaning card is legal (the mortal decides alone) and warns, because the likelier cause is a card whose poleLean names the wrong axis and abstains silently. Near-zero lean resolves by a seeded coin drawn only in that band, so two runs differing solely in how convinced a mortal was stay in step. Absent decidedBy, the branch behaves exactly as before. Zero shipped templates author one yet — THR-883 owns the first choice-designed encounters — so the capability is open and the content has not walked through it.
  • A fork may offer several ways in rather than two ends of one question (THR-898, 2026-07-31). Two-pole mode above asks "which way do you lean?", which is the wrong shape for a route-flavored objective: The Broken Wheel lets a mortal bribe the wainwright, intimidate him, or win him over — three courses, one door, and no single axis whose two ends are those arms. A branch may therefore declare decidedBy: { routes: [...] } instead, each route naming a key, the reach whose capability makes that course come naturally, and optionally an axis + toward pole it also speaks to. Variants key exactly the declared route keys, on the same build-time contract as the two poles and for the same THR-844 reason; validation also enforces unique keys, live reaches, a toward wherever a route declares an axis, at least 2 routes, and at most MAX_BRANCH_ROUTES (6). The dominant term is competence, not conviction — what makes a course someone's is being able to walk it, so the score is capability in the route's reach (via the same computeCapability read resolution itself uses) weighted above the mortal's axis standing and the god's committed cards, each weight a named constant. A card may now lean a route directly (poleLean: { route, weight? }); route leans are unsigned, because a card arguing for "bribe" is not thereby arguing against "intimidate" — it simply says nothing about it — and a route-explicit card abstains from every two-pole decision, since a route key is not an axis and must never be read as one. Leaders within ROUTE_DECISION_TIE_EPSILON are genuinely tied and one seeded draw settles it, drawn only in that band exactly as the pole coin is. A winning route that declares an axis drifts the mortal toward its pole like any pole decision; a pure-competence route drifts nothing, because "they were good at this" is no claim about character. The unleanable warn is per route: a fork where the god can argue for two of three courses is exactly as steerable as it looks on two and silently unsteerable on the third. One tuning note worth knowing before authoring — capability runs through a sigmoid with midpoint 10, so raw domain scores above ~20 saturate and two routes at raw 30 and 24 differ by 0.003, inside the tie band; the range where capability actually separates routes is raw ~4–16. As with two-pole mode, zero shipped templates author a route fork yet (THR-883 owns that content).

Candidate selection — the filter cascade IMPL

  • Which encounter fires where runs through the staged filter pipeline (src/engine/encounterFilterPipeline.ts): awareness + faction awareness → visibility gating → prerequisites → threat floor (currently disabled) → a performance cap of MAX_SCORED_CANDIDATES = 40 before scoring. Scoring (src/engine/encounterScoring.ts) computes finalScore = valuePerTick × desireMultiplier + resonance; results are cached (encounterCache.ts, invalidated on structuralCacheVersion).
  • Candidacy is fully visible — intel/visibility never hides a candidate (rejected 2026-05-07, THR-138); intel may only enrich. Branching candidates get a curator boost and a reserved slot past the cap (BRANCHING_CAP_RESERVE = 3) so an eligible branching template is not starved out by cheaper linear ones — the direct lever against the "0 branching fires" KPI gap below.
  • The cap stage starves whatever is appended last, and one class needed protecting (THR-814, 2026-07-27). phaseAgentDecision merges as [...nearbyEntries, ...dynamicEntries], and the cap fills its 40 slots by walking that list from the head. On a medium map nearbyEntries runs to ~4,650, so the per-agent generators at the tail were unreachable by construction rather than outscored: 0 of 1,263 faction quest and lifecycle entries survived, which is the whole reason guild reputation gain was structurally zero. An entry may now declare personallyOffered — meaning a generator built it for one agent's membership, standing, or rank rather than reading it off the shared location cache — and claims one of PERSONAL_OFFER_CAP_RESERVE = 6 reserved slots, exactly as branching entries do. Survival went 0 → 60.6%, and such entries are competitive once seen: a faction entry ranked first on 40 of the 56 boards containing one. The reserve is a floor, not a takeover — the other 34 slots still go to the world.
  • Every shipped template declares who may draw it (THR-736). actorAffinities is a required field on UnifiedActionTemplate, and the candidate path reads it as an opt-in gate: generateUnifiedCandidates skips the actor-type check entirely when the list is absent or empty, so a template with no affinities was drawable by every actor type — faction, culture, place-spirit and all. 18 templates shipped that way (15 mc.* mercenary-company, 3 encounter.shell_proof.*) because the required-field violation sat inside the red typecheck baseline (THR-489) where the ratchet, which blocks only increases, could not see it. All 18 now declare ['individual'] — mortal-drawn content, matching their source: 'agent' — which narrows candidacy rather than widening it. A registry invariant test asserts the offending-id list is empty, so the field cannot silently go missing again.
  • A carried capability opens the same doors an earned one does (THR-737, 2026-07-26). The prerequisites stage gates templates on requiredTraits and blockedByTraits, and both now consult item-granted capabilities alongside the mortal's own has_trait edges: a trait_grant effect on anything they carry or are bonded to counts. The two directions are deliberately symmetric — a grant that unlocks trait-gated content while leaving trait-blocked content open would be half a gate. Granted capabilities carry no level, so they satisfy a minLevel requirement at GRANTED_TRAIT_EFFECTIVE_LEVEL = 1, matching the default the older grantsTraitWhileHeld path uses. Before this the trait_grant primitive had 22 authored payloads and no production reader at all, so an item promising intimidate changed no decision anywhere; the same aggregate now also feeds spell prerequisites and ambition eligibility. The lookup is resolved lazily and at most once per agent, since this pipeline runs for every agent every tick.
  • The trait gate is now declarable, not only readable (THR-801, 2026-07-26). The two fields the bullet above describes — requiredTraits (a TraitPredicate[], so each entry may demand a minLevel) and blockedByTraits (bare refs, deliberately level-free, because holding a blocking trait at any level blocks) — were read every tick by the prerequisites stage but declared on UnifiedActionTemplate nowhere: a phantom API, evaluated by the engine and authorable by nobody. Any template that tried to declare a gate simply failed to typecheck, and the failure hid inside the red baseline (THR-489) exactly as the actorAffinities one did. Declaring them cleared 13 baseline errors across two production readers — the prerequisites stage and the branchingDistance KPI diagnostic, which reports how many agents a template's gate excludes. Each traitId is a ref resolved on ANY-match (node id, short id, display name, or tag) through the shared THR-786 resolver. Authoring is deferred to WS5 content migration (THR-778), so zero shipped templates carry a trait gate yet and the contract suite is still the only exercise of the path — the capability is open, the content has not walked through it.
  • A guild's senior work now asks what you are to the guild (THR-805, 2026-07-26). The prerequisites stage gained a faction-rank gate: a template whose FACTION_ENCOUNTER_META entry carries a questType of senior, elite, or leadership is drawable only by a member of that faction whose member_of.reputation has reached the tier its authored minRank names (meetsFactionRankRequirement, factionReputation.ts). Non-membership closes it — the one negative answer that is deliberately not fail-soft; unresolvable data (unknown faction, a minRank matching no tier) fails open, because a typo that silently orphans content is the worse failure. Rank is always derived from reputation rather than read off the edge's cached rank, which only refreshes on a tier change. The 123 standard quest/social templates stay ungated for members and strangers alike, so nothing previously reachable closed. This is the reader for a requirement authored on roughly 150 template metas and consulted by nothing: guild rank had been declared per template since the faction slice shipped, and any passer-by standing in the right hall could take a captain's commission. Of the 60 rank-gated metas, 12 are cache-reachable today — the guild tail THR-779/THR-803 rescued — and 6 of those sit behind a reputation floor the economy does not currently reach (THR-810).
  • Which encounters a company may take is a predicate, not a list (THR-74, src/engine/groups/groupEligibility.ts). A template is opened to companies when it sits in a swept family (encounter. / borderland. / monster.), has ≥2 steps, carries at least one iron/stone step, and carries no heart/gold/star step — the three axes a company cannot attend (a private reckoning, a negotiation with named seats, an honour only one champion can win). The sweep runs once at the UNIFIED_ACTION_TEMPLATES derivation, so every consumer sees one merged template object, and content later authored into those families is picked up without editing a registry. Currently promotes 63 templates: encounter 44, borderland 14, monster 5. It is additive — a promoted encounter stays available to the solo agents who could always take it. Party-exclusive content is not produced here: it declares actorAffinities: ['group'] plus minGroupMembers at its authoring site, because "only a company may attempt this" is an authored claim, not a shape the engine can infer.

Resolution: sigmoid → d100 IMPL

  • The "sigmoid" and the "d100" live in two files. The sigmoid produces the capability: src/engine/domainCapability.ts bends a raw per-Reach score through sigmoid(x) = 1 / (1 + e^(−k(x − midpoint))) with SIGMOID_MIDPOINT = 10, SIGMOID_K = 0.4, yielding a 0–1 Domain Capability. The d100 resolves against a linear threshold derived from it (src/engine/resolutionService.ts): probability = capability + sphereFactor − difficulty + modifiers + influenceNudge, clamped to [PROBABILITY_FLOOR 0.05, PROBABILITY_CEILING 0.95]; the threshold is Math.floor(probability × 100) and a seeded roll 1–100 succeeds when roll ≤ threshold. The raw per-Reach score is not the actor's alone: traits and the items they carry feed it additively before the sigmoid (THR-718). A possessed or bonded item carrying a stat_contribution effect adds its per-Reach term via collectStatContributions inside computeRawScore's possesses/bonded_to walk, so a legendary blade can lift its bearer a whole capability tier. Since THR-1096 the same walk also follows accompanies: a Companion — a named person travelling with the mortal, not a simulated agent — contributes their domainContributions through the identical path, so their bonus reaches the roll and earns a factor line under their own name in getTopContributors. Companion contributions are deliberately small (COMPANION_CONTRIBUTION_RANGE, raw 1–3, below the agent-scale band) and always on: they nudge a roll, they do not decide it.
  • Crits are the Phase 2 doubles model: a roll of matched digits (11, 22, … 99; 100 is not doubles) at or under threshold → critical_success, over threshold → critical_failure. A skilled actor (high threshold) catches more doubles under the line, so skill shifts which crits land, not just how often you succeed. An intermediate success_at_cost band also exists.
  • A company answers with its best member, not its leader (THR-74). When the acting agent belongs to an active company and the template is group-eligible, resolveGroupStep substitutes the companion best suited to that step's Reach, and the others contribute a capped assist — assist = min(GROUP_ASSIST_CAP 0.15, n × GROUP_ASSIST_PER_MEMBER 0.05) over companions clearing GROUP_ASSIST_MIN_TIER 2, plus GROUP_BOUND_RESOLUTION_BONUS 0.03 when the company is bound. So a party's expedition is answered step by step by whoever is actually good at that step, which is the mechanical shape of "we each carry our part". The bonus rides the same additive modifier channel as push and the committed nudge hand (not intervention — THR-1121 removed the paid-choice term from that sum). The action's owner is always still the individual — no encounter or awareness system ever meets a positionless group node — and the substitution is visible on the resolution.input trace as groupId/actingMemberId/groupAssistCount/groupBonus. Fail-soft: a disbanded or emptied company resolves as an ordinary individual.
  • Two companies that meet resolve as one contested pair, not two solo rolls (THR-731). When a company's encounter completes on a hex where a foreign NPC band stands, collectBandOppositions gives the band a counter-action and detectContestations emits the pair; resolveContestationPair then rolls both sides independently, each priced through the same resolveGroupStep math as the bullet above — so a six-strong band fights like six, not like its leader. The initiator always attacks (bands answer, they never open the pair), and the defender wins stalemates, which is the shipped stability bias. Winner and loser land on the contested_won / contested_lost bands; those bands shipped with the contested-pair system (TB-044) but had no producer at all until group contests, so a company that lost a fight used to read as one that merely failed. Consequences beyond the step's own effects: GROUP_COHESION_CONTEST_WON_DELTA +0.08 / _LOST_DELTA −0.12, a BAND_CASUALTY_CHANCE 0.35 roll on the losing side, and a mutual hostile_to grudge between the two companies. Fail-soft: a band that dissolved or emptied before resolution degrades the pair to the ordinary uncontested path — conflict never blocks a resolution.
  • Scale tempers difficulty (src/engine/resolutionScaleAdjust.ts): SCALE_DIFFICULTY_OFFSETS (personal −0.20 … cosmic +0.10), MIN_PROBABILITY_BY_SCALE (personal 0.70 … cosmic 0.05), and CRIT_FAILURE_PERMITTED_BY_SCALE (personal/local crit-failure disabled — small acts don't catastrophically backfire). No special-cased entity types — the same machine governs a peasant and an Ascendant (load-bearing decision, CLAUDE.md). Determinism (NFP #3): seeded roll, no Math.random(); traces resolution_input / resolution_result.

The nudge hand DESIGN

  • Schema is additive-optional (THR-773): ActionStep.nudges?: StepNudge[], UnifiedActionTemplate.traitVariants?, UnifiedAction.activeNudges? in src/types/unifiedAction.ts (278 importers — zero signature changes). A step with no nudges resolves byte-identically to the pre-THR-773 path; the whole feature is opt-in. Reads DESIGN rather than IMPL because the engine seam is shipped and tested but no shipped template authors a hand yet — authoring is WS1 (THR-774), the player-facing interface WS2 (THR-775).
  • Scope predicate is the existing attention tier — AttentionTier === 'story_beat', the same signal the curator already sets and traces. Not a second definition of "attended", and not a new tick-loop cost: background/shaping encounters never enter the nudge path.
  • Modifiers are named, not anonymous: each committed card contributes { source: 'nudge:<id>', delta } and each applicable trait variant { source: 'trait:<id>', delta } (collectNudgeModifiers, src/engine/encounters/nudges.ts). They join totalActionModifiers in resolveUncontestedStep — the same additive channel as push and the company assist — so one modifier total feeds the scale adjustment, the probability floor, and the trace. Intervention left that sum in THR-1121 (2026-08-15): a choice's probabilityBoost is no longer read at all, so an authored choice still costs essence and still keys which authored ending resolves, but buys no odds. The named nudge:<id> channel is now the only way a player decision moves a roll, which is the point — it renders as words, and it is authored per card rather than priced per stance. That channel is not clamped (THR-827): computeResolutionThreshold clamps only the summed probability to [0.05, 0.95], never the modifier term, and no caller bounds it either — resolutionModifiers.ts caps each contributor (sphere ±0.10, equipment 0.15, terrain ±0.10, traits 0.10, effects 0.30) and never their total. ResolutionInput.actionModifiers carried a // capped at ±0.20 note that was never enforced and never true; enforcing it would have made cards 3–8 of an authored hand inert. Hand strength is bounded at authoring time instead, by NUDGE_HAND_MAX_TOTAL_DELTA (0.70) in src/data/content-eval/nudgeAuthoringConstants.ts — warn-level and director-tunable, deliberately not a runtime clamp.
  • Riders are pure band-mapping over the full six-value StepOutcome domain (including near_miss) — no_crit_fail maps critical_failure → failure; floor_at_cost maps failure → success_at_cost and near_miss → success_at_cost (a near miss is a failure texture and floors with it); all_or_nothing (The Gambit, THR-885) widens both ends — success → critical_success, success_at_cost → success, near_miss → failure, failure → critical_failure, crits unchanged. The Gambit sits last in NUDGE_RIDER_PRIORITY because it is the only rider that can worsen an outcome: when a hand also holds a protective card, the protection wins. Zero draws from any rng stream, so same seed + same cards ⇒ same d100 and every downstream stream consumer is untouched. A reroll_once rider was designed and rejected at audit for exactly that reason. Strongest single rider wins per NUDGE_RIDER_PRIORITY; they never stack. Applied last, after push/resist/both floors — and the pre-rider outcome is captured first so resistSucceeded is never misattributed to a rider.
  • bandProse keys on StepOutcome — the six-value step enum. Not OutcomeBand (outcomeConsequences.ts) and not the five-band EncounterOutcomeBand; either would type-check while being the wrong domain.
  • Hand partitioning: a trait-gated card the mortal cannot hold is hidden; cost/sphere/unlock blocks dim (the price is information, an impossible card is noise). Fail-soft throughout — unknown rider value, addNudgeIds naming no card, or a committed id with no authored card are each inert with one warn. THR-885 adds two world-state filters on the same hide-not-dim rule: requiresGroup (The Fellowship — dealt only inside a group) and requiresFavor (The Favor's call variant — dealt only when a favor is owed). The rule is that dimming shows a price the player could pay; hiding covers what they could not possibly make true from inside the encounter.
  • Cost channels beyond essence (THR-885): StepNudge.costs?: { detectionDelta?, doomDelta? }, summed across the committed hand and charged once at aftermath through each host system's own API — applyRawDetectionDelta (src/engine/encounters/detectionPressure.ts) and accelerateDoomClock/decelerateDoomClock. detectionDelta is signed, which is why the raw entry point had to exist at all: every prior detection writer priced by EncounterChoiceCost band, and a band can only raise pressure, so The Veil had no channel to write through. Both entry points share the module's clamp, and the emitted trace reports the delta actually applied after clamping rather than the one requested. Summing (not first-wins) is what lets a player pair The Veil against The Heavy Hand and net off; a net-zero channel is not charged at all.
  • Grants reuse the aftermath effect vocabulary (THR-885): StepNudge.grants?: EncounterAftermathReactionEffect[], dispatched through the existing applyEncounterAftermathReaction by dispatchNudgeCommitments (src/engine/encounters/nudgeDispatch.ts), fired from phaseAutonomousAftermath after the encounter's own aftermath so a grant lands on the world the encounter left behind. Its carrier is deliberately not state.emittedOmens: an omen is addressed to a place and catches whoever passes, a compulsion to a person and travels with them, and that difference is the card's printed trade — steer them, not the world.

  • World-state phases are seed producers too, and that is how systemic pressure becomes a scene. phaseRouteEvents (THR-669) plants encounter_route_ambush / _toll_dispute / _route_embargo off cargo manifests, and phaseArmySupply (THR-626) plants army.supply.forage (a strained host), army.threshold.mutiny (a starving one), or army.supply.siege_lifted (a starving besieger) off supply lines derived over road and trades_with conduits. Both follow the same discipline: an anomaly proposes, the seed system and attention budget dispose, and equilibrium produces nothing at all — a fed army and a balanced route are silent. Both are also bounded (one live seed per route/army, a per-scan world-wide cap) so a systemic pressure can never become an encounter stream.
  • Authoring note — a seeded template is not pool-drawn. Templates that fire by lifecycle or phase trigger carry locationSubtypes: [] as the load-bearing signal that they are spawned, not selected from a location pool; src/data/army-encounter-content.ts is the clearest worked example. A corollary worth knowing before authoring more of them: a template with no producer never renders. Four army cohesion-threshold templates shipped in THR-104 whose THRESHOLD_ENCOUNTER_TEMPLATES map has zero non-test consumers — crossing a threshold emitted a notification and nothing else — so the prose sat unreachable until THR-626 gave one of them a seeding path. Author the trigger in the same pass as the template, or grep for the producer before assuming one exists.
  • Hidden marks (HiddenMark, src/engine/hiddenMarks.ts) are concealed consequences planted on an agent — invisible to other agents, held in GameState.hiddenMarks[] (not graph nodes, for cheap querying), revealable by a future encounter matching the mark's revealFamilies (a MARK_REVEAL_SCORING_BONUS nudges those encounters up, THR-112). Consumed probabilistically (REVEAL_PROBABILITY_MULT = 0.9 × severity) and decayed (MARK_DECAY_GRACE_TICKS = 20, MARK_DECAY_PER_TICK = 0.02, dropped below MARK_DECAY_FLOOR = 0.05) in phaseHiddenMarkDecay.ts. Visible to the player only via the Debug Panel, never in normal play — the engine's slow-burn setup mechanism.
  • Reveal families are a declared vocabulary (REVEAL_FAMILY_ALIASES, src/data/reveal-family-aliases.ts, THR-844). Matching runs through familyMatchesTemplate(): a family resolves to one or more template-id prefixes and matches if any of them prefixes the drawn id; an unaliased family resolves to itself, preserving the original raw-prefix behaviour. Before this landed, matching was raw templateId.startsWith(family) and 67 of the 115 authored family literals matched zero templates — including investigation, the corpus's most-used family at 65 uses — so 42 of 136 mark entries were unrevealable by construction: placed, decayed, never surfaced. src/engine/__tests__/revealFamilyLiveness.test.ts now fails the build if any authored family resolves to nothing, if any prefix in the table is dead, or if any mark entry loses its last live family. Note the divergence: encounter-seed encounterFamily is still matched raw and does not consult the alias table.

Aftermath — reactions & afterimages IMPL

  • Aftermath (src/engine/encounterAftermath.ts, plus the aftermath phase) applies the selected Reaction's world-graph mutations via GraphOp: trait writes, reputation shifts, edge mutations, chronicle entries persisted in agent history. Reactions reshape the protagonist. If unattended, the encounter falls into its default reaction.
  • The criticalSuccessAfterimage / criticalFailureAfterimage fields are real in the ActionStep schema, consumed by selectBandAfterimage (src/engine/encounter.ts:100–103) to attach band-specific closing prose to a critical outcome. They are sparsely authored — the subject of THR-584 (author afterimages for the top-20 hot templates).
  • A condition applied by aftermath now actually wears off (THR-761, 2026-07-26). Both condition-minting aftermath effects — apply_condition and condition_attachment — write the has_trait edge property ticksRemaining, which is the field the one tick-driven expiry path (decayConditions, src/engine/conditionDecay.ts) counts down and removes at zero. Previously they wrote only durationTicks, which no production reader consumes, so an authored duration was decorative and every such wound, curse, or blessing was permanent for the rest of the run. The two fields are deliberately kept distinct rather than unified: ticksRemaining is the live counter, durationTicks the authored total retained as provenance and as the denominator for duration UI. A duration of 0 still means indefinite, expressed by omitting ticksRemaining so the decay loop skips the edge. Authors get expiry for free — the effect's durationTicks is now honoured without any extra field.
  • A step outcome can carry aftermath effects directly (THR-783, 2026-07-26). successMetadata.effects / failureMetadata.effects (ActionStepOutcomeMetadata) hold an EncounterAftermathReactionEffect[] applied when that outcome side fires, dispatched through the same applyEncounterAftermathReaction the reaction path uses — so the full effect vocabulary is authorable per step, with no reaction for the agent to pick and no smaller parallel vocabulary. The side split is isStepSuccess, which counts near_miss as a success, so a near miss does not fire failure effects; and because it is per step, a two-step encounter failing both steps applies the effect twice. This replaces onFailureEffects, a key the THR-101 tavern migration authored at five sites in place of the legacy appliesWound flag: it was never declared on the type and never read by any engine module, so from 2026-04-24 until this fix a lost tavern brawl wounded nobody. Caveat closed (THR-809, shipped 2026-07-27): the condition definition nodes are seeded at world construction by seedEncounterTraitDefinitions, so a condition_attachment naming one lands in a live game rather than fail-softing on template_missing. Verified in a seed-42 CLI world (THR-1143).
  • Half the ending is engine-derived, and it now speaks the same language as the authored half (THR-1004, 2026-08-06). An EncounterAftermathChange comes from two places: authored variant prose, and the resolver itself, which mints changes for capability growth, personal reputation, faction standing, reputation tallies, clearance-gate state, and the reward. The derived half used to be built from debug-grade template literals — ${growthApplied.toFixed(2)}, and the raw internal tally key star.positive — which the chip surface then drew faithfully onto a mortal-facing screen, so the words-never-numerals rule was broken upstream of every component that could have enforced it. Every derived sentence is now built by src/engine/aftermathWords.ts, which bands each delta into words the way difficultyWord bands step difficulty and resolves a tally key through the reach vocabulary (star.positive → "reputation for Star"); the numbers stay on the trace for the designer. The resolver is required to route through that module rather than assemble a detail string of its own, which is what makes the numeral sweep in aftermathWords.test.ts a gate over the production sentences rather than a sample of them. The second half is presentation: a change now carries concepts — the game concepts its sentence names, declared by the producer — so the chip attaches each one’s tooltip, its entity tile (EntityVisual) and a link routed by kind (onSelectEntity opens a faction or artifact sheet; an absent kind still means "a person", the narrative linker’s cast scan). A surface cannot honour the UI Law by parsing English, and the linker only finds names it was handed, so declaring them at the producer is the only route that covers derived vocabulary. resolveUnifiedReward returns the instantiated node id alongside the display name, which is what gives a prize chip the item’s own art and a working link instead of a name-hashed fallback tile. Fail-open throughout: an undecorated concept is plain text, a chip whose entity will not resolve draws its kind tag alone.
  • Aftermath can hand a mortal a person (THR-1096, 2026-08-14). grant_companion and remove_companion join EncounterAftermathReactionEffect, minting or removing a Companion from the template library in src/data/companion-templates.ts. A grant is deliberately exempt from the per-bearer cap (COMPANION_MAX, default 3): the reward-pool path enforces it, an authored grant does not, because an encounter that promised someone delivers them and the surface shows the crowd honestly. Loss is a story event either way — removeCompanion always emits companion_departed with a named reason (contract_ended / lured_away / story / bearer_missing) and returns the template's authored departure sentence, so nobody ever silently vanishes. Contracted companions ride the condition-expiry beat via expireCompanions; permanent ones carry no counter and cost nothing per tick. Fail-soft throughout: an unknown template id, a missing bearer, or an already-instanced unique warns once and grants nothing rather than minting a blank node.
  • Aftermath can grant every attachment category the palette rule names — and the agreement path had been built the whole time (THR-1110, 2026-08-14). THR-1082's palette rule calls all seven attachment categories legitimate consequence material, but the effect vocabulary reached exactly two: condition (condition_attachment / apply_condition) and possession (spawn_artifact). blessing, curse, bestowed_power, spell and agreement had no granting member at all, so an author reaching for one had two options: fake it in prose — a chip claiming state nothing wrote, the pathology THR-971's single-sourcing rule exists to stop — or drop the consequence. Measured on THR-1097: A Bargain at the Crossroads is literally an agreement and shipped carried by its encounter_seed, so the promise was a scheduled encounter rather than a thing the bearer held. attachment_grant closes all five, and is a dispatcher, not a new system: instantiateReward already resolved the node-backed categories from the template's own type/subcategory, and instantiateAgreementReward had existed in rewardPool.ts since the attachment-slot design with zero callers — a fully built dormant path. Nothing about how an attachment is written changed; what was missing was an aftermath's ability to ask for one. The category is never declared — the author names a templateId and the template's own shape picks the path, so there is no taxonomy field to get wrong; a dead id is caught at build time by the grant-liveness sweep, which now checks attachment_grant refs against every attachment catalog. Agreements are the one category needing two parties: a condition sits on one person, an agreement is a claim between two, so it is edge-backed (relates_to with agreement: true) and counterpartyId is required — bind it to a scene cast member ($cast:<key>) declared must-persist, because a promise whose holder is collected at scene end is not a promise. A counterparty that does not resolve writes nothing and traces why rather than leaving a dangling edge, so a missing cast declaration surfaces as a consequence that silently never lands. durationOverride beats the template default (null = permanent). One trap when you add an explicit cast: withDefaultSupportBundle returns early on any template declaring a supportBundle, so naming only your new actor silently drops the setting-class default (THR-1044) the encounter was staged with — compose with it rather than replacing it.
  • The scene-targeting sentinel vocabulary is $actor, $target and $cast:<key> — and the third of those was missing for a year (THR-1025, 2026-08-08). bindAftermathSceneTargets rewrites sentinel-valued targetAgentId / withAgentId / targetFactionId / targetSublocationId fields to real node ids before dispatch, binding only when the resolved node's kind matches the field; an unresolvable sentinel is left in place and the effect no-ops down its invalid-target path. It knew $target and $cast: but not $actor — which is the established token everywhere else in the codebase, since resolveRef (src/types/graphOp.ts) maps it for every GraphOp. So content authoring targetAgentId: '$actor' on an aftermath effect was following the documented vocabulary, and the binder silently passed the literal seven-character string through to be consumed as a node id. The consumer's effect.targetAgentId ?? actorAgentId fallback could never catch it, because a non-empty string is not nullish. Seven content sites were affected. Authoring rule: the three forms above are the whole vocabulary — anything else in a sentinel-eligible field is treated as a literal node id, so a typo'd sentinel fails as a missing node rather than as a bad reference.
  • An ending can change a place, not just the people in it (THR-1143, 2026-08-16). The three condition effects — apply_condition, remove_condition, condition_attachment — take an additive targetLocationId, so an aftermath can shut a pass for the season, start a festival, put a square under watch, or blight a harvest. This is a widening, not a new effect kind: the same has_trait edge, the same ticksRemaining counter, the same single decayConditions expiry path, so nothing about authoring a condition changes except who carries it. The starter set is trait.condition.location.* (pass_closed, festival, plague_scare, under_watch, harvest_blight), and $target binds the field when the action targeted a location. Sublocations keep their own field — targetLocationId will not bind a node carrying a parentLocationId, so a tavern inside a keep never resolves to both. Two systems read it, and that is the point: requiredTargetTraits gating (a festival-gated template becomes eligible while the festival runs, and stops being eligible when it lifts) and movement cost, where LOCATION_CONDITION_MOVEMENT_TAX multiplies the price of entering the place — pass_closed at ×8, deliberately a soft block, because a hard one can strand an agent whose only road home runs through the pass. Player-facing, the location detail panel lists what a place is carrying and for how much longer, in words. Authoring note: a condition absent from the tax map costs nothing to travel through, which is the designed default rather than an omission — most of what can happen to a place is not about the road. under_watch is the deliberate example.
  • The ending can reflect how it ended (THR-969, 2026-08-02). AftermathVariant.byOutcome maps a UnifiedActionOutcome to per-band overrides of overview / changes / reactionPrompt / reactions, on a choice-keyed variant or on the fallback. Before it, BranchAwareAftermathConfig keyed only on the choiceId of one step — and because most linear templates ship variants: {}, a choice-less encounter could render only its single fallback, so "crossed clean" and "fell in the river" produced byte-identical aftermath prose and chips by construction. Resolution order is choice → outcome band → base variant → fallback: the choice picks the variant, then the band layers onto that variant field by field, so the two axes compose rather than compete. Keyed on the seven-value UnifiedActionOutcome — not the five-band EncounterOutcomeBand, the six-value StepOutcome, or OutcomeBand from outcomeConsequences.ts, each of which type-checks while being the wrong domain. Optional and additive: an unauthored band, or no outcome at all, degrades to the un-banded variant rather than throwing or blanking, and a band-less config resolves to the identical object it always did. The lookup is a single shared resolver (resolveAftermathVariant, src/types/unifiedAction.ts) called by both the engine's aftermath assembly and the encounter-stage adapter — deliberately one function, because those two surfaces each carried their own copy of the choice lookup and a UI copy that missed the band would render the un-banded ending the engine had already resolved past.
  • An aftermathConfig without a fallback silently deletes the ending, and the type system could not say so (THR-1038, 2026-08-08). BranchAwareAftermathConfig declares branchOnStep, variants and fallback all required, and the resolution order above bottoms out at fallback — so a config missing it has no base to return, and applyAftermathOutcomeBand read byOutcome off undefined and threw on every resolved action. The tick loop's fail-soft envelope swallowed the throw, which is why nothing was red: no crash, no failing test, and the authored aftermath simply reaching nobody. 15 mercenary-company templates shipped exactly that, holding a bare reactions array where the config's three required fields belong — and not EncounterAftermathReaction objects either, but the effects that belong one level further in, under an older key vocabulary (domain/weight for key/delta, label/delay for message). Authoring rule: the canonical no-fork shape is { branchOnStep: 0, variants: {}, fallback: { overview, changes, reactionPrompt?, reactions? } }; reactions live on the variant (fallback.reactions), never on the config, and each reaction is a { id, label, intent?, effects[] } wrapper around the effect list. Nothing in the engine reads aftermathConfig.reactions — every consumer reads aftermathConfig.fallback.reactions. Because unattended encounters fall into reactions[0] (via chooseAlignedReaction), the first reaction is the one that decides background behaviour and should carry the consequences the author means to fire by default. resolveAftermathVariant now degrades to an empty EMPTY_AFTERMATH_VARIANT instead of throwing (NFP #4), but the fail-soft is not the gate: a corpus test in unifiedTemplateAftermathReachability.test.ts resolves every shipped template at every UnifiedActionOutcome and fails on any config missing its fallback, because a resolver that quietly returns nothing hides this defect rather than reporting it. The literals were compile errors the whole time, invisible inside the ~3477-error red baseline (THR-489) — repairing them took the ratchet down 15.
  • A variants entry must be an AftermathVariant, not a map of them (THR-1054, 2026-08-09). Third instance of the family above, and the one the first two structurally could not see: the config is well-formed at the top level — branchOnStep, variants and fallback all present — while the values inside variants carry a second nesting level. Ten hod.* templates authored variants[<stepIndex>][<band>] = { overview, changes }, so resolveAftermathVariant looked up variants[choiceId], never matched a step index, and returned fallback — whose changes: [] was empty on all ten. The lost relic, the cleansed corruption and the delivered judgment were written, committed, and reachable by nobody. The authoring rule the shape violates: variants is keyed by choiceId and its values are variants; a per-band ending belongs on byOutcome, which is the axis those authors actually meant. All ten were re-keyed onto fallback.byOutcome, and that route was forced rather than preferred — the file carries no ActionStepBranch at all, so choiceHistory can never hold an entry at any branchOnStep and variants is unreachable by construction on them whatever shape it is given; re-keying onto invented choice ids would only have produced a second dead literal that type-checks. assertDecidedAftermathReachable cannot catch this class, because it returns early on templates with no decided fork — exactly this population — so the gate is a structural sweep in the same corpus test: every variants entry across all 191 templates must satisfy Array.isArray(entry.changes), paired with a per-band reachability assertion that each authored band resolves to prose distinct from the un-banded fallback (the structural half alone would equally pass for a fix that simply deleted the endings). Found by building an encounter package for every live template rather than for a fixture (THR-1046); the nested literals were themselves compile errors hidden in the red baseline, and repairing them took the ratchet down 4.
  • Reachable is not the same as authored — the band floor is three, including one extreme (THR-1055, 2026-08-12). Making the ten hod.* endings above resolve is what first let check:encounter see how few of them there were: the Composition Contract (COMPOSITION_BYOUTCOME_MIN_BANDS, plan ruling 7) requires success, failure, and at least one of critical_success / critical_failure / success_at_cost, and the ten authored one or two bands with no extreme at all. Before the re-keying the gate reported authors 0 outcome band(s) for every one of them, so an under-authored encounter and an un-authored one were the same reading. Why the floor names the tails specifically: they are the bands a playthrough rolls least, so they are the ones that go unwritten unless a contract asks — and an encounter missing them shows its blandest ending most often and its sharpest never. THR-1054's own verification landed hod.quest.temple_vigil on success_at_cost and correctly rendered the un-banded base ending, which is the fail-soft working and the content gap in the same frame. All ten now carry three or more bands (17 authored here). Authoring note on the layering: because applyAftermathOutcomeBand overlays field by field, a band may restate only what differs — omitting reactions keeps the variant's set, which is the right shape when the tail changes what happened but not what the god can usefully do about it; author a band's own reactions when the ending opens a move the base pair cannot express. Note this is an authoring floor, not an engine constraint: the resolver has always layered whatever was authored, so the gate is the only thing that makes a missing tail visible.
  • Which kinds of consequence an encounter has are drawn, not chosen (THR-1145, 2026-08-17). The palette census behind THR-1141 measured what a menu of capabilities produces under a deadline: nearly every shipped consequence landed on an item, a reputation delta, or a condition, while a dozen live primitives went unused. So the factory rolls instead of offering. At brief time npm run draw:consequences -- <templateId> --reach <reach> draws two consequence families (three at rarityTier ≥ 3) from a reach-weighted table — relationship, companion, standing, possession, condition, knowledge, secret, story_seed, thread, drive, movement, place, membership, omen, formative — and the encounter must wire each in context. The weights carry the reach's flavor; the floor carries the variety: heart draws relationship most often and gold draws possession, but every cell of CONSEQUENCE_FAMILY_WEIGHTS is ≥ 1, so a veil encounter drawing possession is the table asking what a blessing looks like as an object rather than a misfire. A zeroed cell would turn the eight reaches into eight rigid genres, which is the same convergence one level up. The hand is recomputed from the template id, so the recorded consequenceDraw field is a claim check:encounter audits rather than data anyone can edit — it fails a mismatch and fails a drawn family nothing wires, naming the kinds that would satisfy it. Exactly one deviation is allowed and must say why: consequenceSwap: { from, to, reason }, with the traded-in family still holding weight ≥ CONSEQUENCE_SWAP_MIN_WEIGHT in that reach — the valve that keeps a drawn companion out of a scene with no persistent cast, not a second roll. The gate is presence-conditional by design: a template recording nothing is silent, because requiring the field outright would have redded precisely the templates not on the RETROFIT_PENDING ratchet — the handful the factory has already finished — to enforce a rule on the unfinished ones. The draw utility (drawFromTable) is deliberately generic and seeded: the plot-hook table (THR-1147) reuses it verbatim, and it is independent of the weight object's key order so reformatting a table cannot re-roll the corpus.

An unattended beat expires at its deadline IMPL

  • A thread set to auto_resolve carries a deadline, and as of THR-1068 something finally reads it. buildEncounterNotification (src/engine/encounterVisibility.ts) has stamped autoResolveTick = tick + RETINUE_VIGNETTE_TIMEOUT on every such notification since TB-035, and until now every reader treated it as a flag or a label — shouldAutoOpenEncounterNotification read non-null as "do not open now", the veil rendered autoResolveTick - tick as a countdown, the debug surfaces printed it. No tick phase resolved anything when the deadline arrived, so RETINUE_VIGNETTE_TIMEOUT named a timeout that had never fired: measured live at tick 113, three notifications sat 32, 36 and 37 ticks overdue and still pending, with the thread row's badge climbing and never falling. expireOverdueEncounterNotifications now runs in the tick's State Cleanup and marks them resolved.
  • Expire, not queue — and this was a reading of the spec rather than a new decision. The intent was already recorded in four places that agree: the attention mode is named auto_resolve; RETINUE_VIGNETTE_TIMEOUT is documented "Ticks before retinue vignette auto-resolves"; autoResolveTick is "Tick when auto-resolve fires (null for pause mode)"; and THR-880's own comment describes the alternative as "silently auto-resolving after RETINUE_VIGNETTE_TIMEOUT ticks". Nothing in the tree describes an unattended beat as queueing.
  • Flipping resolved is the whole of it, because a notification never drove the encounter. A notification is a player-facing record; the UnifiedAction advances and concludes in the tick pipeline whether or not anyone looks, and an unattended encounter falls into reactions[0] by the existing default-reaction path regardless. Every pre-existing resolve site — disregard, acknowledge-aftermath, intervention commit — writes that same single flag and mutates nothing else. So expiry costs the player the offer to intervene in a moment that has already passed, which is precisely what choosing auto_resolve means, and costs the world nothing.
  • Two exemptions, both deliberate. An aftermath notification never expires by deadline: at this tier the badge is the player's only route into a concluded beat (THR-943), so retiring the conclusion record eight ticks after it appears would delete the sole surface reporting what happened — narrative loss (NFP #5) at the tier that opted out of interrupts precisely so outcomes would be reported. Those records still leave state via NOTIFICATION_RETENTION_TICKS (50), and they keep a non-null autoResolveTick because that field also carries the "do not auto-open" meaning — nulling it would make every aftermath interrupt at the one tier that must never be interrupted. Legacy-sourced steps are exempt for a stronger reason than the one originally recorded here: there are none. THR-1068 excluded them defensively, because generateEncounterNotifications keeps resolved records in its de-duplication key set for unified_action and aftermath but drops resolved legacy ones — so retiring a legacy record while its encounterProgress was still active would re-emit it the next tick, forever. THR-1069 asked the prior question and found the branch unreachable by construction: agent decision takes its legacy arm only when a template resolves through getAnyEncounterById but not through getUnifiedTemplateById, and the latter is index.get(id) ?? getAnyEncounterById(id) — a strict superset — so the unified arm always wins. A 60-tick sweep confirmed it: zero encounterProgress entries alongside 52 unified actions across 33 distinct templates. The guard stays because the superset relation rests on that single trailing fallback, which 36 encounter-pool ids depend on; removing it would revive the branch and make the churn loop real. legacyEncounterBranchUnreachable.test.ts fails the moment that happens.
  • Authoring/verification note. The overdue backlog was not only visual: phaseAscendantHandFilter iterates every unresolved non-aftermath unified_action notification each tick and runs template adaptation plus a forecast computation per record. Note also that __DEBUG.inspectEncounterPipeline reads a GameState snapshot that lags __DEBUG.tick() and returns all notification records rather than pending ones, so a browser reading of "zero overdue" can be a stale-reader artifact; prefer a headless sweep for anything asserting post-batch notification state.

"Dilemma" here is the plain word, not the Dilemma system IMPL

  • The player layer uses "dilemma" descriptively (a hard choice with no clean win). It is not the named Dilemma system, which is a separate divine-meeting mechanism: src/engine/dilemmaSelection.ts selects EnrichedDilemmaTemplates (resonance-scored) for ascendant meetings — a distinct type from UnifiedActionTemplate, not part of encounter resolution. A UL-disambiguation candidate; do not wire encounter aftermath through the dilemma-meeting path.

The four design rules — the executor's contract DESIGN

  • Quoted verbatim from Docs/plans/2026-05-04-encounter-experience-design-plan.md §1 (also in Docs/canon/encounters.md): (1) Path over adjective — every choice changes the path, not the adjective; (2) the moral axis is structural — each encounter's primary Reach maps to an archetype-pair axis (Iron: Protector ↔ Conqueror; full eight in cosmology.md), and each choice tilts the agent toward one pole; (3) verbs are encounter-specific, soft-power flavoured — "Stir her resolve," never full control; (4) every primitive is clickable — every node an encounter names has a detail page. Flagged DESIGN because they are an editorial contract enforced by authoring discipline, not engine-enforced.
  • Player-as-god framing is binding (rejected: player-as-character). All choices are god actions (whisper, steady, withdraw, strengthen); "let them handle it" is always valid. Any choice that makes the mortal the agent is a drift bug.

KPI reality check — the honest gap DESIGN

  • The systems that host the curated chapter are IMPL and rich; the content volume and tuning are catching up. The THR-457 KPI harness recorded a June-11 baseline of ~89% encounter failure and 0 branching fires per 30 ticks.

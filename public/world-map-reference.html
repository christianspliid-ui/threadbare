The World: Hexes, Terrain & Sight

Every mortal, every settlement, every buried ruin sits somewhere. The map is that somewhere — a grid of hexes you can shape, corrupt, and restore, seen through a fog that lifts only where you or your own are looking.

The world is a grid of hexesThe whole world is one field of hexagonal tiles. Each hex is a patch of ground with a terrain, a look, and a mutable state you can change.

Threadbearer's world is a single continuous map of hexes — flat-topped hexagonal tiles laid edge to edge. Every hex has a terrain: grassland, forest, mountain, marsh, desert, tundra, ocean, and a good many more, each with its own colour and character on the map. Terrain is not just scenery; it shapes how hard a place is to cross, what tends to live there, and what a moment set there feels like.

A hex is also more than fixed ground. Beneath its terrain each tile carries a small ledger of mutable state — how much of your divine influence has soaked into it, how much corruption has taken root — and those numbers rise when you act on the land and ebb on their own over time. Enough of either, held long enough, and the very terrain of a hex can change: blighted ground degrades, hallowed ground is restored. The map is a thing you write on, not just a board you play across.

How big is the world

The map's size is chosen at world generation. A medium world is a few hundred hexes across its span; a large world runs to a thousand or more. Bigger worlds hold more mortals and more story, at the cost of a heavier simulation — which is why testing sometimes prefers the medium size.

Where things are — three nested tiersEverything in the world resolves to a hex, but not everything sits directly on one. Position nests: a hex holds locations, a location holds sublocations, and a mortal stands on exactly one rung.

The world's places are arranged in three tiers that nest inside one another. This is how the game answers the simple question where is this? for every mortal, settlement, and encounter.

HexThe tile itself. The coarsest unit of place — a patch of the map with terrain and state. Everything ultimately resolves to a hex.
LocationA named place on a hex. A village, a keep, a shrine, a ruin. A hex can hold several. This is where most of the world's life happens.
SublocationA place inside a place. The tavern within the town, the vault within the keep. The finest grain of position, nested one level below a location.

A mortal always stands on exactly one of these tiers at a time — pinned to their most specific place, whether that is a sublocation, a location, or (out in the wild, with no built place around them) the bare hex. When any system needs to reason about distance or sight, it reads a mortal's position and follows the nesting upward: a sublocation sits inside its location, which sits on its hex. Two mortals in different taverns of the same town are, to the world's geometry, standing on the same hex.

Why the tiers matter to you

When you drill into the map — hex, then location, then sublocation — you are walking down these same tiers. And because everything resolves to a hex, the game can answer "who else is here?" and "what can this person see?" cleanly, no matter how deep inside a place someone is standing.

The land remembers what you do to itDivine influence and corruption are the two forces that mark a hex. They build when acted on, fade when left alone, and — past a threshold — remake the terrain itself.

Two opposed pressures write themselves onto the land. Divine influence is the imprint of your hand — where you have blessed, warded, or worked wonders, the ground carries a glow of it. Corruption is the opposite stain, spread by dark workings, festering ruins, and the slow rot of an unmaking world. Both are held on the hex, and both decay a little every turn: influence fades faster than corruption, so a blessing is a thing you must renew while a blight lingers.

When either pressure is pushed high enough and held there, the hex transforms. Corruption soaked past its threshold degrades a hex's terrain toward something ruined; sustained divine influence past its own, higher threshold restores it. A hex remembers its original ground beneath the change, and once it transforms it will not transform again until a cooling-off period has passed — so the land shifts in deliberate steps, not a flicker every turn. This is the slow, visible way a god reshapes a corner of the world.

Magical saturation — a place's charge

Built places carry one more kind of charge: magical saturation, the concentration of arcane energy at a location. You raise it by warding a place or founding a seat of power, and by working sphere-aligned wonders there; left alone, it bleeds away each turn. A saturated place makes magical acts easier, draws the arcane-minded toward it, and widens what can be sensed nearby. Saturation belongs to the location, not the raw hex — it is the mark of a place, where influence and corruption are the mark of the ground.

Sight, and the fog over the worldYou do not see the whole world at once. Fog covers what no one of yours is watching — and, separately, each mortal sees only so far across the hexes themselves.

A god is not omniscient here. The map wears a fog of war, and a hex you have never watched sits unexplored — dark, unknown. You lift the fog by looking: through your avatar where it walks, through the mortals of your retinue out in the world, and through scrying a distant thread. Wherever one of those eyes reaches, the hexes around it turn visible, and you see what is truly there, now.

Look away and the world does not vanish, but it goes stale. A hex you have seen and then left slips to remembered: you keep the picture of it as it last was — the places, the faces — but you are no longer told what changes there until you look again. The fog is not ignorance so much as old news. Your sight is narrow and your attention finite, and the map quietly reminds you that ruling by watching means always choosing where not to look.

The mortals have their own sight

Your fog is one thing; what a mortal can perceive is another. A mortal is aware of everything on their own hex automatically — see the hex, see everything on it, every location and sublocation and unfolding encounter. Beyond their own hex, how far they notice depends on their Domain Capability: a capable, worldly mortal senses opportunities several hexes off, while a green one barely sees past their own doorstep. Awareness is measured in hexes, plainly — not in tangled paths between places.

Designer Notes

Hexes as flat array state (not graph nodes), the three-tier located_at position model, per-hex terrain transformation, per-location magical saturation, the two hex-distance sight systems (player fog of war vs. agent encounter awareness), and the per-tick hex→actor index — with honest IMPL/DESIGN status.

Status flags follow the rulebook convention: IMPL shipped and wired, DESIGN specified but partially or not yet wired, OPEN an unresolved question (referenced, never minted here). Canon authority for this page is Docs/canon/hex-map.md; terminology authority is the UL Docs/ubiquitous-language/Graph.md (HexTile, TerrainType, Three-tier Position Model, located_at Edge, Hex/Location/Sublocation). Where a term here and the UL disagree, the UL wins and this page is a drift bug.

Load-bearing: hexes are not graph nodes IMPL

  • Hex mutable state lives in GameState.tiles[], a flat array — never the graph. The world graph holds nodes (locations, sublocations, actors, artifacts…) and typed edges; hex tiles are plain array state indexed by coord.{col,row}. A HexTile carries terrain, baseTerrain, divineInfluence, corruption, explorationAttraction, and terrainTransformedTick (src/types/index.ts). This is a settled decision (CLAUDE.md § Load-Bearing Architectural Decisions) — do not model hexes as nodes.
  • Hex actions therefore do not mutate the graph. They produce HexMutation[] (src/types/hexMutation.ts: {col, row, field, delta}) accumulated on state.pendingHexMutations and applied in phaseHexState.

Three-tier position model IMPL

  • hex → location → sublocation. An actor occupies exactly one tier via a single located_at edge pointing to the most specific node they stand on. Resolution is upward: a sublocation carries parentLocationId → parent location → hexCol/hexRow → hex. All spatial reasoning resolves to the hex level.
  • Reference resolver: resolveLocationToHex(graph, locationId) in encounterAwareness.ts — recurses through parentLocationId for sublocations, then reads hexCol/hexRow; returns null (fail-soft) when coordinates are missing.
  • Honest note on the dual representation: the canonical relationship is the located_at edge (used by visibility.ts and encounterAwareness.ts), while hexActorIndex.ts reads the mirror property actor.properties.locationId to build a fast per-tick index. Both resolve up to the same hex; the edge is the model of record.

Hex state tick — terrain transformation IMPL

  • phaseHexState(state, pendingMutations) (orchestrator, mid-tick) runs once per tick, pure over state.tiles. Per hex: apply pending mutations → decay divineInfluence by HEX_DIVINE_INFLUENCE_DECAY_RATE = 0.02, corruption by HEX_CORRUPTION_DECAY_RATE = 0.01 (and explorationAttraction at the influence rate) → terrain transform checks → emit a hex_state trace per meaningfully-changed hex. All-zero, unchanged hexes short-circuit.
  • Transformation: corruption ≥ HEX_CORRUPTION_TRANSFORM_THRESHOLD 0.7 degrades terrain (via getTerrainTransformation('corruption', terrain)); divineInfluence ≥ HEX_DIVINE_TRANSFORM_THRESHOLD 0.8 restores it — degradation wins if both fire. baseTerrain preserves the original ground; HEX_TRANSFORM_COOLDOWN_TICKS = 10 gates re-transformation. Transformation table: src/data/terrain-transformation-content.ts.

Magical saturation — per location, not per hex IMPL

  • phaseMagicalSaturation(state) decays the magicalSaturation property on location nodes (mutates graph node properties in place, like reputation decay) by MAGICAL_SATURATION_DECAY_RATE = 0.02/tick, clamped [0,1]; emits a saturation_tick trace per changed location. Zero-saturation locations short-circuit.
  • Saturation is driven up by other systems (Ward, Place of Power, sphere-aligned encounters); this phase is only the natural decay. High-saturation effects (magical-action scoring bonus above MAGICAL_SATURATION_VEIL_BONUS_THRESHOLD 0.5, Veil-reach attraction, widened visibility above …VISIBILITY_THRESHOLD 0.3) are applied by their own consumers — a location-scoped charge, distinct from the hex-scoped divineInfluence/corruption.

Player fog of war IMPL

  • recalcVisibility(prev, sources, graph, tick, cols, rows) (visibility.ts, inline near the end of runTick) recomputes a VisibilityMap keyed "col,row" over the whole grid. Three states: unexplored → visible (in range of any LOS source) → remembered (was visible, now out of range; a StaleSnapshot of location + agent names is captured at tick − 1). Newly-visible hexes auto-reveal the land layer.
  • collectLOSSources gathers line-of-sight from three origins — the avatar (AVATAR_SIGHT_RANGE = 0), retinue agents on non-dormant threads at tier ≥ 1 (AGENT_SIGHT_RANGE = 1), and scry targets (SCRY_SIGHT_RANGE = 0) — each range passing through getModifiedValue(…, 'los_range', …) for effect modifiers. Ranges are deliberately tight: sight is narrow, and the fog is a real constraint, not decoration.

Agent encounter awareness — hex distance, not location hops IMPL

  • Separate from player fog: filterByAwareness(entries, agentId, agentLocationId, graph, …) (encounterAwareness.ts) decides which cached encounters a mortal can perceive. Same-hex entries (distance 0) are always visible — see the hex, see everything on it. Cross-hex visibility uses hexDistance against a per-reach hop budget.
  • computeAwarenessHops(capability, reach): below AWARENESS_THRESHOLD 0.05 → 0 hops (own hex only); otherwise BASE_AWARENESS_HOPS 1 + floor(capability / CAPABILITY_PER_HOP 0.15), capped at MAX_AWARENESS_HOPS 5. Best of the entry's primary/secondary reach range wins; map-edge hexes get EDGE_HEX_AWARENESS_BONUS 1, plus any range_modifier effect bonus.
  • Rejected: location-hop awareness (BFS over the distance-matrix via adjacent edges). It was inconsistent (sublocations invisible, same-hex vs cross-hex ambiguous, irregular topology). Hex distance is geometric, predictable, and sublocation-agnostic — the awareness oracle. The distance matrix still exists for encounter scoring, but is not the visibility source.

Per-tick hex→actor index IMPL

  • buildHexActorIndex(graph) (hexActorIndex.ts, THR-188) builds a pure, module-state-free Map<"col,row", string[]> of the individual actors on each hex, resolving each actor's locationId (up through parentLocationId for sublocations) to hex coordinates. Unresolvable actors fall to unresolvedCount for a fail-soft trace. Iteration follows stable insertion order for determinism. getActorsOnHex(index, col, row) reads a bucket.

Tick placement IMPL

  • Mid-tick: phaseHexState (applies pendingHexMutations, then clears them) → phaseMagicalSaturation. Late-tick, after phases settle: inline recalcVisibility + land auto-reveal. buildHexActorIndex and filterByAwareness are helper calls invoked where encounter/awareness scoring needs them, not scheduled phases.

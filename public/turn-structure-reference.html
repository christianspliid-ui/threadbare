The Three-Beat Turn

The world does not run on a clock of its own. It waits. It moves only when you choose to look, to act, and to let the consequences land — and it always moves in that order.

The turn is yours to advanceThis is a turn-based god-game. Time does not pass until you say so.

Nothing in the world happens behind your back on a timer. The simulation is turn-based: the clock is frozen until you advance it, and every advance is a deliberate act. You are never rushed by a ticking wall-clock, never punished for pausing to think. A god watching from a height has all the time in the world — the world is the one that runs out of it.

Because you hold the clock, the tempo of a session is yours. You can sit for a long while inside one mortal's crisis, or sweep through several quiet ticks where you do nothing at all. Doing nothing is always a valid move. Restraint is not passivity for a god — choosing not to intervene is itself an intervention, and the game treats it as one.

Scan → Curated Moment → AftermathEvery turn is a rhythm of three beats, always in this order.

A turn is not a single click. It is a small arc of three beats — you look, you enter a moment, you live with the result. Each beat has a different feel and a different demand on you.

Beat One

Scan

You look across your portfolio of threaded mortals. The world surfaces signals — a thread under stress, an encounter cresting, a tug on your attention — but whose story to watch is your choice. The scan is what makes you a god looking down rather than a reader turning pages.

Beat Two

Curated Moment

You enter a mortal's situation. A framed encounter opens — concrete, particular, grown from this mortal's own entanglements. You read the prose, weigh the options, and at the turning point choose a divine nudge from a menu shaped to the moment. Or you choose nothing.

Beat Three

Aftermath

The moment resolves. Threads thicken or fray, the world shifts, and closing prose ends the chapter. This beat asks the least of your hands and the most of your attention — you sit with what happened before you look again. Then time advances, and the next scan begins.

What an agent carried answers back here too: on the outcome an item may snap, spend itself, mend a wound, or lay a curse — keyed to where the roll landed on the ladder, and usually chanced rather than certain. Those consequences arrive as their own line of prose in the aftermath, so a blade breaking is a beat you read, not a number you notice later.

Why the order is load-bearingThe three beats are not interchangeable. The sequence is the design.

The beats always run scan, then moment, then aftermath — and each boundary carries a meaning that would be lost if you rearranged them.

Scan before the moment

Means you chose to look here. The encounter you enter is one you selected out of many — not one the game shoved at you. Agency lives in the gap between all the stories and the one you opened.

Moment before aftermath

Means every consequence is anchored to a moment you witnessed. The world does not mutate in the abstract; it mutates because of a chapter you were present for.

Aftermath before the next scan

Means the world has already moved by the time you look again. You do not get to freeze it between turns — you are a god, not an editor with an undo key.

Read together, the order encodes the whole premise: a being who chooses to attend, witnesses what unfolds, and lives with a world that keeps turning whether or not it is watched.

How time passesWhen you advance the turn, the world moves by a fixed, small amount.

Each advance moves the world forward by one tick — two in-world hours. Twelve ticks make one in-world day, so a single day is a dawn-to-dawn ribbon of twelve steps you walk through at your own pace.

Dawn ··· +2h +4h +6h +8h +10h Midday ··· Next Dawn

The tick is the world's heartbeat and the unit every other system beats to — needs stir, threads settle, clocks creep, encounters ripen, all measured in ticks. A tick is short enough that a day has real texture and long enough that you are not micromanaging minutes. You are watching a world unfold, not steering a character through a schedule.

Encounters are chapters, not turnsThe curated moment is the game's chapter unit — and you author how many by how many threads you weave.

It would be easy to imagine an encounter fires on every tick. It does not. The curated moment is a real chapter with a beginning, a choice, and a consequence, not a slot machine you pull each step. But their density is largely yours to author: a quiet portfolio goes many ticks between chapters; a god with a dozen threads in motion generates them far more often. The world adds a gentle lean toward more chapters as the doom clock climbs — a crumbling world crests more crises. The game never rations chapters. Between them are quieter ticks where you scan, tend your portfolio, and let the world breathe.

A note on density

Encounter density is player-authored, with a gentle world-pressure lean toward more as doom climbs (CURATION_PHASE_MULTIPLIERS — set to flat to disable). "Three to six per session" is retired as doctrine; it survives only as an untuned observation about a comfortable single sitting. The real design problem is not how many — it is reading many at once, answered by the Chapter Ledger: every encounter, running or finished, stays fully readable for the whole run. What remains open is only whether the game should offer a soft stopping-point signal, pending real play data.

The point of the frozen clock, the deliberate scan, and the chapters you choose to enter is the same: to make each moment you do enter feel chosen. A god who could see everything at once and act every tick would be a spreadsheet. The three-beat turn keeps you a witness with a hand — looking, choosing, and living with it.

The turn is turn-based — single-step contract IMPL

  • The world advances exactly one tick per player command. runTick(state, scryTargets, runtime): GameState in src/engine/orchestrator.ts is a pure single-step function — it takes a GameState, runs one full tick, and returns the next GameState. Nothing in the engine auto-advances the clock; the caller decides when to step.
  • Each tick begins by cloning state with tick: state.tick + 1 and clearing tickEvents. Every phase is a pure function that takes state pieces in and returns partial updates the orchestrator merges — the "beat contract" the player experiences is the front-of-tick framing over this phase pipeline, not a separate scheduler.
  • "Do nothing is always valid" is structural, not a special case: advancing a tick with no divine action queued simply runs the world's own phases (agent decisions, clocks, decay) and returns. The five verbs stay implicit substrate (rulebook §4, Open Q #2 resolved) — there is no "pass turn" action to fire.

Tick granularity IMPL

  • TICKS_PER_DAY = 12 (src/data/attention-constants.ts:14) — one tick is two in-world hours; twelve ticks make one day. This is the load-bearing pacing constant every time-based system beats to (needs, decay, clocks, encounter ripening).
  • Time-of-day and day-count are derived from the tick counter, not stored separately — determinism (NFP #3) means the same tick index always maps to the same in-world hour.

Scan / portfolio surface DESIGN

  • The Scan beat leans on the attention system, which is partially wired: ATTENTION_BASE_CAPACITY = 6 (src/data/attention-constants.ts:19) is the base pool, but the full portfolio surface — attention tugs, thread-stress signals, the whole scan UI — is in progress under the Attention Tier Model project. The beat contract is stable; the surface that dresses it is not fully built. See the attention-story page (W16) for the deep treatment.

Encounter density DESIGN→IMPL

  • Encounter density is player-authored (more threads woven and interventions made → more chapters) with a gentle doom-phase lean (CURATION_PHASE_MULTIPLIERS, branchingConstants.ts; set flat to disable). The game never rations chapters — settled 2026-07-04, THR-603. "Three to six per session" is retired as doctrine.
  • Rulebook Open Q #4: the density half is now resolved; only the stopping-point signal half remains deferred to KPI data (last_considered: 2026-07-04) — a soft "good place to stop" nudge, once the THR-457 harness yields real session data. This page references that open question; it does not mint a new one.

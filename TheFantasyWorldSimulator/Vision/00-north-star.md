# North Star — what the game feels like at its best

> Written as the designer watching over a prospective player's shoulder. If a feature does not move the session closer to this, the feature is probably wrong.

## The moment we are building toward

It is the seventh or eighth hour of their second run. The player has a handful of mortals they know by name — not because the UI tells them, but because they have watched these people *choose* things across dozens of ticks, and the choices have accumulated into something the player has opinions about.

One of those mortals is about to do something the player does not want them to do. The player has a small supply of divine essence and a menu of interventions: a whisper, a nudge, a vision. Any of them will cost. None of them guarantees the outcome. The mortal is not a unit. The mortal is a person the player has come to care about, and whose sovereignty the player has been trying to respect because that is the kind of god they decided to be this run.

The player hesitates. They use the intervention. The mortal still makes a choice — the intervention shifted the odds, not the outcome — and the choice hurts. The player sits with it. They do not reload. They watch the story unfold from here, because the *unfolding* is why they are playing.

That is the session we are building toward. Everything else is scaffolding.

## What has to be true for that moment to land

**The mortal has to feel like a person, not a unit.** That means they have to have had interior life the player witnessed — a favor fulfilled, a grudge carried, a bond formed — before the crisis arrived. If the player meets them for the first time in the crisis, the moment does not work.

**The intervention has to feel consequential in both directions.** Cost, yes — essence spent. But also *moral* cost — the player chose to override another being's sovereignty, and the game never lets them forget that choice is a *kind of being*, not a neutral tool.

**The outcome has to feel partially in the player's hands and partially not.** Pure randomness reads as slot machines. Pure determinism reads as solved puzzles. Threadbearer lives in the middle: the player shifts probabilities, the world resolves, and sometimes the world resolves against them.

**The player has to want to see what happens next.** Not "what do I do next" — the protagonist question — but "what does the *world* do next". The player is a god looking down at a living thing. The pleasure is witnessing, not steering.

## The texture of a good run

A good run has **cadence, not pacing**. Long stretches of simmer broken by set-pieces. The simmer is where the threads get woven — small encounters, small decisions, small ripples. The set-pieces are where the player spends essence and learns who their mortals really are.

A good run has **one complex story at a time.** The player has a portfolio of mortals, but at any given hour there is *one* who is front-of-stage, and the game knows it. The scan → encounter → aftermath loop exists to make sure one mortal's story is legible before another's takes focus. This is Malazan, not a 4X — we want the feeling of a chapter, not the feeling of a spreadsheet.

A good run has **weight of threads.** Every attachment the player has — every bond, every favor, every grudge they helped spin — is load-bearing. Losing a thread should hurt. Keeping too many threads should become a problem the game surfaces, not a number the player optimizes.

A good run ends with **a story the player can tell in prose**. If the player finishes a run and the best they can say is "I got to X mechanic by Y tick," the run failed its own pitch. If they can say "I watched Serafina break with her brother over a promise I made her keep, and I still don't know if I was right" — that is the product.

## What a good run is NOT

It is not a campaign the player "completes". Threadbearer does not reward completion; it rewards attention. The player who saw one mortal's full arc is having a better time than the player who touched fifty.

It is not a puzzle with a right answer. There is no correct intervention at any given tick. The game does not rank the player. The game gives the player the tools to be a *kind of god* and then shows them what that kind of god produces in a world.

It is not a story engine the player reads. The player acts. Divine interventions shift the world. If the player is just watching text scroll by, the loop is broken — we have become a Twitch stream of our own simulation. The player's hand must be on the tiller.

It is not a game about winning. It is a game about *watching something you care about and choosing what kind of being to be toward it*. The endgame is reflection, not victory.

## The emotional register

The master tonal principle (see `Systems/Tonal Bible.md`) holds here: **nostalgic form, adult substance**. The surface looks like a cozy fantasy strategy game — hex grid, signifier art, warm palette. The substance underneath is adult: moral ambiguity, consequences that do not reverse, sovereignty and consumption as the central axis, mortals whose lives we are shaping without their consent.

Cosmic melancholy is the baseline. Wonder layered over grief. The player should be able to laugh and cry in the same session.

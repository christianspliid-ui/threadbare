Three Approaches — The Unchanging Signs Behind Every Culture's Names

A · Abstract Geometric

Pure shapes — alchemical, astronomical, pre-linguistic. These feel like symbols carved into the fabric of reality before any culture existed to name them. Maximum universality — no culture can claim these shapes as "theirs." The cost: less immediately readable. The player learns them over time, like a new alphabet.

B · Stylized Pictographic

Simplified but recognizable objects — instantly readable even at first encounter. These feel like tarot card symbols or heraldic charges. The trade-off: they carry cultural association (swords are "human," scales are "mercantile") which slightly undermines the culture-neutral ideal. But they teach themselves — a new player knows what the sword means immediately.

C · Hybrid — Geometric Frame + Iconic Element

A consistent geometric frame (the "divine constant") containing a single recognizable symbol. The frame shape is always the same for each domain — it's the shape the god-player sees. Inside it, a simplified icon provides instant readability. This gives us both: the culture-neutral geometry AND the "I know what that means" pictograph. The frame also works as a standalone at small sizes.

Cultural Names — Same Icon, Different Words

The icon and color are the cosmological truth — what the player-god sees. But every culture in the world names the domains differently, just as real cultures name the same stars differently. When the player zooms into a culture's perspective, they see that culture's vocabulary. The UI can show the local name with a colored icon pip to maintain readability.

Notice: The dwarves call Shadow "Soft-Tongue" — a derogatory term revealing their cultural contempt for intrigue. The goblins confuse Star and Gold ("Shiny") because their culture doesn't distinguish spiritual and material wealth. The elves have compound words that encode philosophical meaning. These naming differences ARE worldbuilding — they tell you everything about what a culture values and despises, without a single line of exposition.

The Land

A high flat expanse above the world, wind-scoured and remote. The plateau drops away on all sides into dry canyons whose walls show the strata of ages — red clay over pale chalk over dark stone that no one has named. The air is thin and restless, carrying dust from the east and silence from everywhere else. Those who live here answer to no lowland lord.

The land offers little comfort but much endurance. Wind-blasted stone breaks through the thin topsoil everywhere, and the few rivers that survive the climb run fast and cold through narrow gorges. What grows here grows tough — scrub grasses, thorn-bark, and the pale lichen that the canyon-folk boil into dye.

The Soul

Energy runs strong through this high place — not as fire, but as the relentless crackle of static charge that builds before storms break over the plateau. Lightning strikes are frequent, and the canyon-folk say the stone itself remembers each bolt. At dusk, thin golden threads pulse in the exposed rock faces where veins of quartz catch forces older than the sky.

Faintly, beneath the dominant charge, Time pools in the canyon depths. The strata in the cliff walls are not merely geological — they glow faintly orange at certain hours, concentric ripples of temporal residue from an age when something here measured the turning of the heavens.

The People

The Children of the Shifting Dry Canyons claim this wind-scoured height — a people shaped by patience and territorial pride, who mark their boundaries in hammered iron and sun-bleached bone. Their ways tend toward the chaotic: fluid hierarchies, personal honor over written law, and a deep distrust of any structure that outlasts the generation that built it. They venerate Energy above all else, reading omens in the lightning and forge-fire alike.

Yet it is The Iron Covenant that holds the gates. Their banners hang from every approach to the Forge of Sorrow — the single town on this plateau, built around a forge whose fires have not gone out in living memory. The Covenant's grip is administrative, not cultural; they tax the pass roads and maintain a garrison, but the people's loyalties run deeper than any banner.

In the Forge, one figure draws the eye: the Hollow King, who operates on careful reciprocity — what he receives, he returns in kind, and nothing more. The canyon-folk treat him with a respect that stops just short of reverence, as though he is a fixture that makes the settlement itself seem intentional.

The Ruins

Before the Canyon people came, this plateau belonged to the Star-Readers — a people of chaos and celestial obsession who charted the heavens from this high, clear vantage. Their name survives in the region itself: the locals call this expanse the Storm-Born Reach, a corruption of a Star-Reader phrase that meant "the place where sky touches stone."

Shattered observatories still dot the plateau's eastern edge, their great lenses cracked, star-charts etched into stone floors now open to the sky. At certain hours the Time-residue in the canyon walls aligns with scoring on these observatory floors, and the charts glow faintly — a dead culture's instruments still measuring a sky that has moved on without them.

They read the heavens until the heavens read them back.

The eastern observatories have not been fully explored. Something in the lowest level still draws power.
